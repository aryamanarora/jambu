# Telugu metathesis: analytical casebook

Research snapshot: 8 September 2026. This supplements the essay **What moved in
Telugu metathesis?** It records analyses of existing evidence, not corrections
installed in Jambu's lexical graph. A DEDR number identifies an etymological
group; it does not assert that all its words have one inherited stem.

**Notation.** Stars mark proposed forms. An arrow between starred forms is an
analytical step, not an attestation. `ẓ` represents the consonant written `ḻ`
in ordinary Tamil transliteration and `r̤`/`ṛ̆` in some Jambu sources; it is
distinct from `ṟ`, the historical alveolar stop/rhotic series. `V` is a vowel,
`N` a nasal, and `P` a stop. A final `V` leaves a derivative's vowel unspecified
rather than inventing a universal change between Telugu endings.

**Source keys.** K61 = Krishnamurti, *Telugu Verbal Bases* (1961), especially
pp. 51–68 and notes pp. 129–131; K80 = Krishnamurti, “A Vowel-Lowering Rule
in Kui-Kuvi” (1980); K03 = Krishnamurti, *The Dravidian Languages*
(2003); PSS83 = Subrahmanyam, *Dravidian Comparative Phonology* (1983),
especially chapter 16. Exact source links and access notes are in `bibliography.md`.
The comparative observations below also use the identified DEDR groups.

The [Kui–Kuvi lowering audit](#lowering) separately reviews all 18 proposed
positive examples, the controls, and their etymological dependencies. It is
also available as `vowel-lowering-audit.md` in the downloads.

## Six derivations that discriminate between analyses

### 1. New: DEDR 2149

**Evidence:** Telugu `krotta`, modern `kotta`; Tamil `koẓuntu` ‘tender shoot’;
related uncontracted `koṛ-` formations in Gondi, Konda and Kui. Jambu Telugu
form: `f_ti4dmnqgr73ha`.

**Schematic history:** `*koẓ-utt-V → *koẓ-tt-V → *kẓott-V → *krott-V`,
followed by attested `krotta → kotta`. This exposes weak-vowel loss,
displacement, positional `ẓ → r`, and later `r` loss. The `tt` is already
in the proposed derivative; this is not evidence that every metathesis doubles
a following consonant. K03 p. 159 uses the immediate `*koẓ-tt-` input;
p. 198 gives the longer formation. These need not be conflicting analyses.

**Limit:** Tamil's `nt` derivative is not the Telugu `tt` derivative. Deriving
Telugu directly from the Tamil surface word would invent an unsupported
`nt → tt` change. The short/long root allomorphs and kinship derivatives in
the larger group are not all instances of this same chain.

### 2. Tree: DEDR 4711

**Evidence:** Telugu `mrānu` (`f_cqc6qamwv6hmy`) and `mānu`
(`f_rirwcupcemybq`); Konda Araku `marán` versus Sova `mrānu`; final `n`
elsewhere in the family and in Old Tamil `maran`, beside `maram`.

**Minimal correspondence:** `*mar-an- → *mrān-`, with the Telugu ending and
later `mr → m` treated separately. Literal `*mraan-` plus contraction and
`*marán- → *marā́n- → *mrān-` are possible implementations of the long
outcome; the latter is motivated by Konda, not independently attested in
ancestral Telugu. K03 pp. 150, 160–162.

**Limit:** No Tamil-to-Telugu `m → n` operation is required. Modern Konda
dialects are evidence for a mechanism, not a direct time series through which
every Telugu word passed. Final `n/m` belongs to a wider nominal history.

### 3. Finger: DEDR 5409

**Evidence:** Telugu `vrēlu` (`f_5krorzllosgw6`) and `vēlu`
(`f_m4k2ln4qkit3k`); Tamil `viral`; Kannada `beral`-type formations.

**Minimal correspondence:** immediate `*wer-al- → *wrēl-`, then the Telugu
ending and `wr → w`. An earlier `*wir-al → *wer-al` lowering can precede
this, but the deeper high vowel is disputed. K03 pp. 101–106, 483; K61
pp. 61–62, 67; PSS83 chapter 15.

**Discriminating observation:** deleting the first vowel of `*weral` without
prior assimilation gives the wrong quality. Assimilating the second vowel
to the first and then deleting the first gives the right quality without a
literal swap. Neither analysis makes Tamil `i` an independent proof of
Proto-Dravidian `i`, because earlier southern `i/e` neutralization intervenes.

**Limit:** Other branches' `-and/-anj` formations are not the same derivative
as `-al`. A root reconstruction should not erase this difference.

### 4. Blind: DEDR 1787

**Evidence:** Tamil `kuruṭu`; Kannada `kuruḍu`, `kuḍḍu`; Tulu `kurḍu`;
Telugu `gruḍḍi` (`f_ndolyy4ysamdc`), `gruḍḍu`, `guḍḍi`
(`f_d5e6rlvcbj4uw`).

**Schematic short branch:** `*kur-uḍ-V → *kur-ḍ-V → *kru-ḍḍ-V`, plus
initial `k/g` variation and later rhotic loss. Medial `ṭ` has already acquired
its voiced reflex before the displayed input. Strengthening could instead
precede displacement; the schematic sequence does not establish its order.
The final `i/u` formations are retained as variants, not derived by a free
vowel-substitution rule. K61 §§1.142–154; K03 pp. 130–131, 157–162.

**Discriminating observation:** two original `u` vowels do not force `ū`.
The independent shortened Kannada/Tulu forms support investigating an
earlier consonant group. They do not independently prove all Telugu stages
or decide whether contact contributed to this particular formation.

### 5. Swallow and sink: DEDR 4866 and 4993

**Swallow:** Telugu `mriṅgu` (`f_7dp2pr3nbysj6`) and `miṅgu`
(`f_vsrzcq3kxyfta`), Tamil `miẓuṅku`, Konda `miṛiṅ-` and `ṛiṅ-`.
The conventional short analysis is `*miẓ-uŋk- → *miẓ-ŋk- → *mẓiŋk-`;
nasal–stop voicing and postconsonantal `ẓ → r` give the `mriṅg-` type.
Telugu later loses `r`; Konda can lose the first member instead. A retained
`NP` cluster needs no extra gemination. K03 pp. 161–162.

**Sink:** Telugu `bruṅgu` (`f_d2g74plrs2woe`), Kannada `muẓuṅgu/muẓgu`,
Tulu `murku-`, and Kui `bṛuḍga` support comparison with `*muẓ-unk-`.
The same weak-vowel-loss branch now starts with **identical `u…u`**.
This directly exposes the inadequacy of obligatory identical-vowel
lengthening as a complete output prediction. Contraction followed by
shortening before `ŋk` remains a viable alternative, so the short Telugu
vowel alone does not prove early syncope. The `m → b` change is separately
recognized by K61 p. 28 §1.69;
it is not ordinary voicing and is not automatic before `r`.

**Limits:** The wider ‘sink’ group includes uncontracted, assimilated,
nasalized and differently derived forms. They cannot all be produced by
substituting a different first consonant into one chain. K61 pp. 61–64
is more explicit about immediate derivative structure than the shorthand
in K03 Rule 20.

### 6. Descend: DEDR 502

**Evidence:** Tamil/Malayalam `iẓi`, Kannada `iḷi`; Telugu `ḍiggu`
(`f_mpyejvd67v4ti`), `ḍigu` (`f_tdj2gm3oxspxu`), `digu`
(`f_dwgcjswhqoozu`), and a separate `ḍindu` formation.

**Source's analysis:** `*iẓ-g- → *ẓi-gg- → ḍiggu/ḍigu → digu`;
compare `*iẓ-nd- → *ẓi-nd- → ḍindu`. K61 p. 52; K03 p. 189.
This distinguishes displacement, singleton strengthening, positional
`ẓ → ḍ`, later initial `ḍ → d`, and optional degemination. The two
derivatives are not one word with freely exchangeable `g` and `nd`.

**Loan test:** Kolami `ḍig-/ḍigg-` contrasts with unshifted Parji `iṛv-`
and Ollari `iṛg-` / Gadaba `iḍg-` types. The proposed early Telugu borrowing should
be evaluated against that local contrast, not inferred solely from initial
`ḍ`. K03 p. 162. Different transitive formatives in `dincu/dimpu` do
not license a general `p → c` rule.

## Two difficult liquid comparisons

**An older published alternative matters.** K61 p. 445, entry 901,
derives ‘burst’ `prēlu` from `*per-al < *pir-al`, compares Gondi ‘burst’,
and refers to `pridulu` (entry 898), whose comparison includes Tamil `piri`
‘separate’. K61 p. 437, entry 859, separately discusses `pēlu` with Kolami
`pēl-` and the Tamil lateral group, noting a root-quantity problem and
allowing descent from `prēlu` as an alternative. These page images were
checked directly. The `*per-al` chain fits the narrow displacement rule;
its input is a published cognacy proposal rather than direct attestation.
Gondi/Konda rhotics do not settle the older consonant, because those languages
also have rhotic reflexes of `ḷ` (K03 p. 153). This is a stronger and more
specific alternative to examine than an unspecified ‘different formative’.

For ‘burst’, Malayalam `piḷar-` supplies a **second liquid**, which is useful
evidence but still not a solved Telugu input. Relating its `ḷ…r` to Telugu
`r…l` would require a separately established liquid-order change or another
derivative before the ordinary displacement. This is a testable alternative
to added `r`; inventing a `*peḷal-` stem solely to produce `prēlu` would not
be independent support. The homophonous ‘prattle’ group 4430 must also remain
distinct until any analogical influence is demonstrated.

For ‘nail’, PSS83's exceptional `*okar` should be compared with K03's
separate regional formations on p. 484: southern `*ukir`, `*gōr/*kōr`
in South-Central/Central Dravidian and Brahui, and Kurux–Malto `*or-kk-`.
DEDR has Brahui `hōr`, Kurux `orᵒx`, Malto `orgu`. These prevent treating
the entire problem as a recent isolated Telugu *k*-metathesis, but do not
themselves establish one proto-vowel or a unique history of the consonant
orders. Contact must be evaluated across the full distribution.

| **Tulu/Irula and southern aphaeresis:** `nilā → nlā/lā`, `pilā → plā` types | Similar onsets can arise independently by first-vowel loss, sometimes involving nasals. PSS83 pp. 246–248. | The output spelling alone is not a South-Central inheritance marker. |
| **Kota glide movement:** selected `Cy → yC` | PSS83 pp. 81–87 supplies a distinct change with lexical, phonetic and morphological restrictions. Older Indo-Aryan `cōym`, `caynt` contrast with proposed later Badaga loans retaining `Cy`. | Modern `y` should not be collapsed with the Telugu historical apical class; loan labels cannot be inferred solely from rule failure. |
| **Koṇekor Gadaba:** `savl-e-n` ‘I chewed’, `saluv` ‘chew!’, `saluv-da-n` ‘I will chew’ | Medial reordering combines with vowel insertion. Bhaskararao's chapter in Steever 2020, pp. 408–410, specifies labial `u` versus non-labial `i` for anaptyxis. | The paradigm shows the relevant suffix contrast; it does not require the same first-two-syllable collapse as Telugu. The prose's wording of the metathesis environment is awkward, so the actual contrasting forms are the safer evidence. |
| **Kunha/Kisan:** Kurux `nalakh`, Kunha `nalkhu` | The 2025 field study records local final-consonant/vowel reordering. | Modern Kurux is not an attested ancestor, and other vowel or suffix differences in the published pairs are not explained by reordering alone. |
| **Kondh stop clusters:** `kp/pk`, `gb/bg` | Garrett and Blevins 2009 §22.4 offers an analogical analysis tied to inflection/derivation. | An account of these stop clusters does not disprove Telugu apical displacement. |

## Borrowing controls

* **Persian/Arabic loan vocabulary:** Brown 1903 p. 569 records
  `trāsu ~ tarāsu ~ tarāju` ‘balance’; Krishnamurti's Telugu chapter
  (in Steever 2020, p. 261) lists `trāsu` among medieval loans. The cluster does not
  prove inheritance. The `ā` already occurs in an uncontracted variant.
* **Recognizable Sanskrit material:** DHARMA INSTelugu00101, the land
  donation by Viṭrāju, line 9, reads `druggādēvi` and discusses metathesis of
  `Durgādēvi`. The isolated spelling and its geminate do not establish
  continued productivity of all the prehistoric Telugu rules.
* **A proposed loan with unresolved quantity:** DHARMA's editor also cites
  `uramu ~ rommu` ‘chest, breast’; Brown 1903 labels `uramu` Sanskrit
  (p. 168) and derives `rommu` from Sanskrit material (p. 1096). His latter
  entry actually prints Telugu `ఉరుము` (`urumu`) as its source spelling,
  so it should not silently be treated as exact independent attestation of
  the other citation form. Reordering alone does not explain `o`, the short
  vowel, or `mm`. An input with early lowering plus a consonantal formative,
  or later quantity restructuring, is possible but not established here.
  This is a published loan analysis with an explicit residual, not a fully
  verified prehistoric derivation.
* **Possible early Telugu exports:** ‘tiger’ 692 and ‘descend’ 502 support
  evaluating loans which preserve initial retroflex `ḍ` before its dental
  merger in Telugu. Regional unchanged reflexes and derivative correspondences
  matter more than the onset alone.
* **Possible Kannada imports:** K61 pp. 65–66 proposes borrowing of some
  voiced-geminate formations before they underwent Telugu displacement.
  Relative ordering of nasal–stop change and vowel loss is the argument;
  the presence of a geminate is not sufficient.
* **Marathi donor history:** Southworth's table compares Old Marathi
  `ḍoi` ‘head’ (1278) with the `*kuṭak(k)a → *kḍōka → ḍōka` ‘pot’
  history, and Old Marathi `ḍāv` ‘left’ (1290) with Telugu `ḍā`.
  Innovations can indicate a South-Central-type donor without identifying
  modern Kui or Telugu as the immediate source. The left-word vowel remains
  a residual in the present Telugu account.

## What would change these conclusions?

1. Independently attested eligible **uncontracted Kui–Kuvi `e…a/o…a`** forms
   with established derivative vowels would help discriminate the two lowering
   chronologies. Surface `a` in a different derivative is insufficient.
2. Early spellings or paradigms distinguishing **consonant strengthening before
   versus after displacement** would resolve the short-branch ordering more
   directly than Sanskrit-influenced doubled spellings alone.
3. A complete derivative comparison for **prēlu, gōru, vrīlu, mrēṅgu** could
   either extend the core on independent grounds or demonstrate distinct
   histories. Inventing the exact missing segment that each rule requires
   would not provide such a comparison.
4. Loan stratification needs independently dated attestations, donor
   correspondences, semantics and geography. A binary changed/unchanged
   classifier would lose information essential to deciding inheritance.
5. Jambu should retain source uncertainty, distinguish the historical level
   of each reconstruction, and represent roots, derivatives and compounds
   separately. This would remove manufactured exceptions before stronger
   sound laws are tested.
