The most useful question about Shinaic accent is not simply whether Sanskrit accent survived. It is **which words followed which history**. An inherited long vowel, a newly lengthened vowel, an inflected noun and a compound can arrive at different accentual outcomes for independently identifiable reasons.

This post distills an etymon-by-etymon study of **1,686 families and 25,505 database records** across [Shina](language:Sh), [Palula](language:Phal), [Kalkoti](language:Kalk), [Kundal Shahi](language:Kund), [Brokskat](language:bro), [Sauji](language:Sv) and [Ushojo](language:Ush). The seven claims below have different domains and different levels of historical certainty. Each has ten annotated family examples; those illustrations are **not** the samples from which the charts were calculated.

**Reading the notation.** In Shina and Palula, **E** means early accent, illustrated by *áa*, and **L** means late accent, *aá*: the first versus second mora of a long vowel. Both occupy the same syllable. **Penultimate** means the second-last syllable. Kalkoti **H1/H2** indicate High tone on the first/second mora in the 2023 analysis; its **Low** is a separate property. A stress mark in another source does not automatically encode these distinctions. An unmarked word is not automatically toneless.

Use the chart controls to compare counts and proportions, and where available to change the source or grammatical scope. Exact tables remain readable without JavaScript. The [complete research package](/research/shinaic-accent/quantitative-2026-09-09.zip) contains the final report, all dossiers, annotations and reproducible analyses; [Appendix F](#appendix-f) explains the evidence limits.

<h2 id="inherited">1. Gilgit preserves inherited root-versus-ending accent in a restricted noun class.</h2>

In ordinary nouns with an old long root vowel, an accented root tends to give modern **E**, while an unaccented root followed by a lost accented ending tends to give **L**. In the selected [Degener Gilgit](ref:degener-shina2008) sample, **all 17 scorable families agree: seven root-accented → E and ten ending-accented → L**.

The qualification matters. The study first screened a broader set, then examined **29 explicitly enumerated nominal families** with old a-stem noun meanings and corresponding modern noun senses. This follow-up is not an exhaustive census of a-stems or an independent held-out validation. It includes old-input alternatives such as arrow and flesh. Pooling sources and lects reintroduces discrepancies; the chart makes both the restricted and broader results available.

- **[House](entry:4336):** early Kohistani/Guresi versus late independent Gilgit readings may reflect a real inflectional-class or lect difference. A conflicting Schmidt table also needs resolution.

- **[Smell](entry:4531) and [home village](entry:6547):** Palula has early, raised vowels against the proposed ending-accented ancestors. Retraction or a different formation must precede raising; neither exact history is established.
- **[Foam/steam](entry:9108):** Strand’s early “foam” and Liljegren’s late “steam” share a raised vowel. Lexical splitting or subsequent accent reassignment is plausible, not proven.

<h2 id="gender">2. Short gender endings favor penultimate accent in Gilgit and Palula.</h2>

The clearest large grammatical pattern concerns **accent location**. All **110 scorable Gilgit short-*o* adjective types** have penultimate accent. In Palula, all **37 scorable umlaut *u : i* adjective types** do too. The broader short-*u* adjective sample has **111 penultimate, nine final and six unscorable types**. Selection includes accented final *ú/ó*, so final-accented counterexamples are not filtered away.

The shared location can coexist with different mora alignment: Gilgit *taáto* and Palula *táatu* both accent the syllable before the gender ending. They disagree over which half of its long vowel bears the accent. Historical suffixes, analogy and subsequent vowel developments therefore need separate treatment. Forms come from [Liljegren’s dictionary](ref:liljegren); the morphological interpretation follows [his 2016 grammar](https://langsci-press.org/catalog/view/82/85/399-1.pdf), especially pp. 119–123 on gender inflection and extended adjectives.

```chart

```

Palula forms below give masculine and feminine together. A dash means that no matching Gilgit example was selected for this table, not that the language lacks a cognate. The chart’s seven-language view uses a separate set of core property families, not an expansion of the POS-tagged sample.

{{TABLE:gender}}

**Notable exceptions:**

- Two **age comparatives**, *ghaḍeerú* “older” and *lhookeerú* “younger,” have ending accent, as do their feminine counterparts. An older *gaḍhḗro* citation still conflicts with that pattern.
- Four **quality pro-adjectives in -eeṇú** form another identifiable final-accented group.
- The **tailless compound** *lameeṭičhinilú* has additional morphological structure; **kirpuṭú** remains unresolved. **muṣṭú** is treated as an adverb in the grammar, making its adjective tagging problematic.
- In the separate cross-language property screen, Ushojo *'kinono* “black” has earlier, antepenultimate stress; older Sauji “heavy,” “white” and “red” have final prominence. These prevent extending the Gilgit pattern indiscriminately across Shinaic.

The [exception ledger](/research/shinaic-accent/tables/exception-ledger.tsv) preserves the individual cases. Modern *-u/-o* alone does not prove that every word descends from the same old suffix.

<h2 id="lengthening">3. Aspiration helps predict accent when Palula lengthens short a.</h2>

Biori supplies a useful comparison for Ashret long-*aa* words: a corresponding **short-*a* closed monosyllable**. Partition those Biori inputs by *h*/aspiration before looking at Ashret accent. Without aspiration, **25 of 26 lexemes have E**; with aspiration, **seven of nine have L**.

This is a strong tendency, not an exceptionless sound law. Its value is that the proposed conditioning is independently visible in the comparative form. The **35-lexeme** count collapses the article and numeral “one”; the inventories contain 36 closed form pairs and 37 pairs across all domains. These are alternative counting scopes, not interchangeable denominators. [Liljegren’s historical analysis](https://doi.org/10.5617/ao.5341) provides the quantity and aspiration context.

```chart

```

{{TABLE:lengthening}}

**The three residual lexemes:**

- **[Today](entry:242), *aǰ → aáǰ*:** L without aspiration. Old *adyá* supplies an independent source of final prominence, but its preservation through the quantity changes, or exceptional high-frequency behavior, remains hypothetical.
- **Mountain, *khaṇ → kháaṇ*:** E despite aspiration. Alternative quantity and formation histories need independent evidence; neither the [kháṇḍa comparison](entry:3792) nor the competing [skandhá association](entry:13627) should be selected just to fit the accent.
- **[Grape](entry:6628), *dhrac̣ → dhráac̣*:** E despite aspiration. Old *drā́kṣā/drākṣā́* and competing quantity histories make an older-long route plausible, but not established.

“Hand” and “bone” add a grammatical control: late citations *haát, haáḍ* have early inflected *háata, háaḍa*. The citation pattern is not an invariant accent assigned to every cell of the noun.

<h2 id="raising">4. Early-accented long vowels systematically raise in the Biori–Ashret comparison.</h2>

Vowel quality supplies evidence about the timing of accentual change. In the **final closed syllable**, the reviewed pairs give **early Biori aa → Ashret oo in 29 pairs, aa → uu in six, and ee → ii in eleven**. All 46 early pairs have a raised outcome, although *oo* and *uu* require different histories.

The two late-*aa* pairs retain *aa*, but both instantiate one accusative ending: they are weak controls for a general claim about all late vowels. Thirty further pairs lie outside the closed-syllable domain. Dictionary variant recording also affects which comparisons are available. The chart is a correspondence inventory, not a balanced experiment.

```chart
raising
```

{{TABLE:raising}}

**Exceptions to the narrower aa → oo mapping:**

- **[Father-in-law](entry:12753), [four](entry:4655), [story](entry:12748), [stream](entry:6429), lap and Drosh** have *uu* instead.
- Earlier *au* is supported most clearly for father-in-law by internal *ša(w)úra*. Four and story also occur in the [published proto-au proposal](https://doi.org/10.5617/ao.5341).
- Extending *au* to stream, lap and Drosh only because they have *uu* would make the explanation circular. Their precise histories remain open.

This correspondence constrains the exceptions in §1. Early raised *díiš* “home village” requires an early accent stage before raising, if its proposed derivation is correct. Late raised *phiíṇ* “steam” may preserve the vowel quality of an earlier early-accent stage. Vowel quality can therefore distinguish an older accent reassignment from a very recent one, even when its cause remains unknown.

<h2 id="inflection">5. Palula i-declension inflection places accent on final -í across different citation accents.</h2>

All **115 comparable full inflected forms**, representing **95 operationally distinct lexemes**, accent final *-í*. Within the **aa / ee+i subclass**, all 73 comparable pairs also have late citation accent. The further 42 pairs include both E and L citations, as well as short vowels: suffix accent generalizes more widely than citation L.

This gives counted “night” a coherent grammatical explanation. *raát/reetí* denotes a counted night-and-day period; ordinary *róot/róota* belongs to another declension. The productive aa/ee class also includes *kitaáb/kiteebí* “book,” *gilaás/gileesí* “glass” and *pruugraám/pruugreemí* “programme.” It cannot be a collection of individually preserved Sanskrit accents. The classification is grounded in the recorded paradigms and [Liljegren’s discussion of i-declension and loan umlaut](https://langsci-press.org/catalog/view/82/85/399-1.pdf), pp. 81 and 117–118.

```chart

```

The table samples ten distinct linked families across the two domains. The recent loans just mentioned illustrate productivity but are not invented old etyma to fill out the table.

{{TABLE:inflection}}

**Exceptions and exclusions:** No comparable inflected form in this inventory contradicts final *-í*. Two retrieved pairs are unusable: Biori “rainbow” *niildhráal* was supplied with *izreeṇí*, the inflection of its synonym *izraáṇ*; Biori “gun” *thupéek* was supplied with *toobakí*, belonging to *toobaák*. Their early citation accents remain recorded, but the mismatched pairings cannot test a paradigm rule. Suffix-only shorthand was not expanded by copying the citation’s accent.

The modern class explains why counted “night” behaves regularly now. It does not establish when or how that noun entered the class.

<h2 id="dras">6. Dras plural -eh attracts final accent much more consistently than plain -e.</h2>

The reviewed [Dras grammar and vocabulary](ref:rajapurohit2012) supplies **287 plural citations**. Final accent is marked in **57/60 -eh/-eɦ citations**, compared with **24/176 plain-e citations**. The remaining groups contain 15 *-i* and 36 other formations.

Those totals include double-accented citations explicitly. They count what the source marks, not a reconstructed mora contrast or 287 independent etyma. Candidate singulars were reviewed, but a shared gloss alone does not establish a complete singular–plural paradigm. The contrast supports suffix-conditioned stress while leaving the source’s internal inconsistencies visible.

```chart

```

Five examples below mark final accent with *-eh*, including two double-accented forms; five mark stem accent with plain *-e*. Historical family links identify the lexical comparisons, not direct derivations of modern plural stress from Sanskrit.

**Notable exceptions and source conflicts:**

- **[Knife](entry:2860):** one printed entry has stem-accented *kəɽá:reh*, another final-accented *kʌɽa:réh*. Both count; the regular-looking token cannot erase its counterpart.
- **People:** the stem-only *-eh* citation differs from the grammar’s additional final acute.
- **Scissors:** the stem-only *-eh* form might involve a lexicalized compound, but that explanation is unproved.
- **Brush and hammer** genuinely have two acutes in print. Nail also has a spacing issue, described in its example row.
- **Egg, spindle and dates** have further source-internal paradigm or transcription differences, preserved in the [reviewed paradigm table](/research/shinaic-accent/tables/dras-paradigm-review.tsv).

<h2 id="numerals">7. Ten-compounds and twenty-based numerals follow different Palula–Kalkoti accent correspondences.</h2>

The simple correspondence “Palula E → Kalkoti zero/default High” has several failures. Two, twelve and thirteen, belong to a larger **independently identifiable compound series**. All eight ten-compounds **11–18** have E in the recorded Palula dialect counterparts but **H2** in Kalkoti. The twenty-based forms **19 and 20** instead show **Palula L → Kalkoti H1**.

The contrast survives different modern syllable counts. Twelve and thirteen are short words; other members retain more of the compound. Nineteen is especially informative because its twenty element follows the behavior of twenty itself. The Kalkoti transcriptions follow [Hultman’s 2023 numeral table](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2%3A1772061/FULLTEXT01.pdf), Table 16, p. 28.

```chart

```

{{TABLE:numerals}}

**Exceptions and limits:** There is no conflicting outcome within this observed 11–20 comparison. That does not make eight ten-compounds eight independent innovations. Inherited compound-specific accent and later remodeling remain competing historical explanations. The [seven-language numeral inventory](/research/shinaic-accent/tables/numeral-series-records.tsv) also preserves other formations and source systems.

**[Village](entry:4368), *ghróom/draám*, and [man](entry:9828), *míiš/meéš*, remain outside the numeral explanation.** A lost extra ending, analogy, contact or another formation might account for their Kalkoti H2, but none is independently established. Palula “man” is early *míiš*; changing its transcription to late would falsely make the correspondence regular.

These seven patterns suggest a partial chronology: inherited accent and grammatical formation supplied different starting points; lengthening and raising modified their outcomes; productive inflection and compound organization produced further regularities. The strongest rules apply to specified classes and sources. The remaining exceptions help identify which parts of that history are still missing.

<h2 id="appendix-a">Appendix A. Kalkoti final-vowel loss: ten matching cells, nine families.</h2>

Where Palula retains an **unaccented short final vowel after the accented syllable**, the reviewed Kalkoti comparisons have final High in **all ten scorable cells**. Ten more are unscorable and eight are excluded. Grandfather and grandmother share one broad family, so the ten matching cells represent **nine etyma**. This is a promising correspondence with too few distinct families for a ten-etymon main section.

{{TABLE:apocope}}

The table preserves unmarked source variants alongside marked forms. **For knee, grandfather, grandson and aunt, the tonal evidence comes from their explicit placement in Liljegren’s 2013 rising-contour classes**, not from an acute on the displayed citation: *pheep* is specifically low-rising/glottalized. An unmarked spelling contributes no extra tonal confirmation. “Long” has short-vowel final High, while “horse” combines final High with Low. Cow *ghaáu* is a vowel sequence, and boy *phoó* has an accented long final vowel: neither is a transparent member of this lost-short-ending class.

Without the ending, the E → zero/default comparison has seven matches, four final-High residuals, fourteen unknown and ten excluded cells. The L → H1 comparison has four matches, four Low interactions, four unknown and one excluded. Wheat, belly, sister and salt supply the Low complications. Older analyses sometimes represent a falling contour with late Low where the 2023 analysis uses early High; that is not an observed sound change between publication dates. See [Liljegren 2013](https://journals.dartmouth.edu/journals/xmlpage/1/article/423?htmlOnce=yes) and the [complete comparison table](/research/shinaic-accent/tables/kalkoti-palula-combined.tsv).

<h2 id="appendix-b">Appendix B. Gilgit cluster reduction and late aa: three clean comparisons.</h2>

Three particularly clear examples have late long *aa* before an unaccented ending. They suggest a local quantity history rather than a general accent outcome for every reduced consonant cluster.

| Etymon · meaning | Gilgit | Comparison and annotation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [álpa](entry:722) · few | *aápo* | Old short a plus lp contrasts with the modern late long vowel; the final adjective ending remains unaccented. |
| [áśru](entry:919) · tear | *aá~ṣo* | Cluster restructuring and new length accompany late accent. The nasalization is part of the recorded outcome. |
| [taptá](entry:5679) · hot | *taáto* | Astori *tátto* retains a geminate comparison; Kohistani *táto*, Guresi *tátu* and Palula *táatu* show other quantity outcomes. |

The broader short-*a* retrieval adds *jhalla → jeél* and *manyú → moón*, making five late outcomes, but the extra vowel/glide developments prevent treating all five as one demonstrated compensatory-lengthening process. Extending the rule to every old short vowel fails on early “camel,” “poor” and “cat.” Fig belongs to an independently recorded **long-vowel phālgu branch**, and *baáṣ* means **lung**, with a disputed etymology; neither is clean evidence for this rule. The [cluster screen](/research/shinaic-accent/tables/short-root-cluster-summary.tsv) keeps the wider controls visible.

<h2 id="appendix-c">Appendix C. Kundal Shahi has five unexplained postaccenting nouns.</h2>

The source actually inspected was **Rehman and Baart’s 9 January 2004 draft**, not the unretrieved final 2005 publication. Its relevant inventory has **21 paradigms: seven fixed, two mobile rising, seven truncated feminine formations and five postaccenting nouns without rising stems**.

{{TABLE:kundal}}

The five nouns share an inflectional pattern. Ox and shoe have long roots, so a blanket “short roots attract suffix accent” explanation fails. A floating accent can describe the pattern, but does not establish its historical source. By contrast, the seven truncated feminine paradigms preserve fuller *-ii* forms that independently support a lost/shortened ending analysis. All cells, source tables and page locators are in the [complete Kundal Shahi paradigm inventory](/research/shinaic-accent/tables/kundal-primary-paradigms.tsv); its database gloss candidates are not asserted new ancestry links.

<h2 id="appendix-d">Appendix D. Sauji may have restructured pitch accent, but unmarked records cannot prove it.</h2>

[Gomes’s acoustic study](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2%3A1055060/FULLTEXT01.pdf) argues against a Palula-like lexical pitch-accent contrast in **71 nouns from one male speaker**, using basic and inflected forms in isolated and framed conditions. That is evidence compatible with loss or restructuring, with an explicit sampling limit.

[Knobloch](https://urn.kb.se/resolve?urn=urn:nbn:se:su:diva-182519), p. 16, explicitly did **not** investigate stress and pitch accent. Reviewing all **573 records from that source** cannot turn their unmarked forms into 573 demonstrations of tonelessness. Older final-accented “heavy,” “white” and “red” also differ from the mainly prefinal property pattern elsewhere.

| Evidence | What it supports | What remains unestablished |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acoustic sample: 71 nouns, one speaker | No Palula-like lexical contrast identified in that sample | A complete Sauji tonal inventory, dialect coverage and the historical route of change |
| Knobloch wordlist: 573 records | Segmental and lexical comparisons | Tone categories for unmarked words |
| Older accent-marked property words | Recorded prominence in those source varieties | Whether disagreement reflects sound change, lect, speaker or transcription |

A stronger historical test needs matched cognates, paradigms and comparable recordings across speakers and varieties. Brokskat and Ushojo similarly contribute useful syllable-location evidence without supplying a complete mora-accent history. Source notation and access levels are documented in the [source register](/research/shinaic-accent/tables/source-register.tsv).

<h2 id="appendix-f">Appendix F. Complete evidence, counting units and reproducibility.</h2>

The study froze its analysis on **9 September 2026 before composing the final report**. Its 25,505 raw records yield **25,593 analysis tokens** after documented splits. The 1,686 families cover the research-linked portion; **13,270 raw records remain unlinked**. All linked families have a first-pass dossier, but source and record verification are uneven: family-context review is not an individual printed-source check.

The classes overlap, so their counts must not be summed into a grand total. A **family** can contain multiple formations and meanings; a **lexeme** can have multiple forms; a **citation** can be duplicated or conflict with another source. The numerals share morphology, the adjective samples contain related types, and some vowel pairs instantiate the same grammatical ending. The charts report descriptive counts, without treating these as independent random observations or adding misleading significance tests.
