## Kalkoti: agreement and disagreement between analyses

Hultman pp. 15–18: distinguish unmarked VV, H on first mora V́V, H on second VV́,
syllable-wide L V̀V, and L+H V̀V́. This gives up to five surface patterns from two
tone types and position, rather than five unrelated primitive tonemes. Words may
have no H or more than one H (míšaalúm ‘men, oblique’). **Unmarked transcription
can mean uncertainty**, especially 2006 recordings. Hultman found 2015 recordings
easier; consultant JK spoke slowly and hyperarticulated. Do not count all unmarked
dictionary forms as demonstrably toneless.

Table 9: Kalkoti daan, raat, čoor, bii ~ Palula dáand ‘tooth’, ráat ‘blood’,
čúur ‘four’, bíi ‘seed’; Kalkoti šáak, púu, róo ~ Palula šaák ‘wood’, poó ‘boy’,
rhoó ‘song’; Kalkoti taár, deéd, yeé, dríg ~ Palula tóoru ‘star’, déedi
‘paternal grandmother’, yéei ‘mother’, dhrígu ‘long’. meéš ~ míiš ‘man’ is an
explicitly unexplained counterexample to the new-apocope explanation.

**Do not explain taár as simple survival of an old accent on lost -u.** Palula
tóoru was accented on its first mora, with unaccented final -u. Liljegren 2013
p. 143 proposes replacement of old mora accent by default stress, then new L
from aspiration and new H from apocope/glottalization (p. 144). He proposes a
glottal intermediate with rising pitch as its phonetic effect. Hultman instead
recognizes a remaining early-H class corresponding to Palula late accent, so a
blanket claim that ALL inherited accent disappeared is too strong. A possible
model is loss of old early H, retraction/retiming of old late H, plus new final H
after apocope, but the three late-to-early lexical comparisons are insufficient
to establish a universal law. Must test further. 2013 šaàk has final L attributed
to right-edge aspiration; 2023 šáak has early H. This is a source/analysis
discrepancy, not proven change between recording dates.

L correspondence: bùun ~ bháanu ‘becomes’ versus unmarked buun ‘goes’; dä̀rin ~
dharaáṇ ‘ground’; ḍä̀är ~ ḍheér ‘belly’; ǰä̀gär ~ ǰhangaár ‘liver’; gìin ~
ghíinu ‘took’; lùun ~ lhoóṇ ‘salt’; yùun ~ yháandu ‘comes’; ä̀sil ~ hansílu
‘laughed’; dùúr ~ dhúura ‘far’; gòór ~ ghúuṛu ‘horse’. Voiceless aspiration
survives and causes predictable low pitch, so no extra lexical L is necessary
there. mh/nh/rh in Palula mhaás, nhiáaṛa, rhoó have no L in Kalkoti maas,
niyär, róo. Hultman suggests Gawri influence. Could also be Palula innovations
in aspiration or earlier loss in these onset classes; check comparative history
before labelling every such word a loan. ràat ‘night’ has L despite no Palula
aspiration; Gawri has L too, but direction of transfer still requires evidence.

Utterance-final H on the final vocalic mora is realized as creak/glottalization
(often with final sonorant devoicing); /traál/ → [traaˀɬ], but /traál=ää/ retains
the rise before the question clitic. Short múr may have both H and glottalization.
This is present-day allophony. It is compatible with either historical direction
between H and glottalization; the synchronic alternation alone does not prove the
glottal stop was the first cause of the tone.

## Main Shina and Brokskat

Schmidt & Kaul 2008 p. 234: Gilgiti, Kohistani, Guresi, Astori and Drasi have
pitch-accent transcription. **Brokskat has stress-only transcription**: falling
contours were heard on some long vowels but no rising contour was detected, and
list intonation could not be excluded. Thus lack of demonstrated lexical contour
contrast, not equal experimental proof of complete loss in every Brokskat variety.
Unaccented long vowels documented in Kohistani, not the other listed Shina dialects.

Pp. 244–245 and fn. 17 (images checked): Gilgiti bat ‘rice’ considered Kashmiri
loan; Astori baát is regular from bhaktá > *bhatta > baát. Most rising long vowels
described as secondary lengthening after cluster reduction, e.g. taptá > *tatta
> taáto ‘hot’. Old accented long ā has early accent, e.g. bhrā́tṛ > ẓáa ‘brother’.
Palula táatu versus **Gilgiti** taáto is evidence for different timing/type of
lengthening; needs morphological accent reset and cluster history made explicit.
The source table has Kohistani **táto**, Guresi **tátu**, Astori **tátto**.
Thus a useful hypothesis is earlier *tátto followed by different treatments of
the geminate: retained Astori, simplified without lengthening Kohistani/Guresi,
compensatory lengthening with late accent Gilgiti, and simplification followed by
independent open-syllable lengthening with early accent Palula. This is NOT a
uniform all-Shina rule. Do not confuse the article's illustrative Gilgiti reflex
with the reflexes of every dialect.

## Kohistani grammar preview, directly inspected 8 September

Schmidt & Kohistani 2008 pp. 15 and 24–28 were read as page images in the
publisher-authorized Google Books preview. Page 29 hit the viewing limit; no
attempt to bypass it. A complete research copy was subsequently found in the
user's Downloads directory (see the fuller evidence below).
https://books.google.com/books?id=mZzD86umtbAC&pg=PA24

- p. 15: historical OIA voiced aspiration lost, but a voiced-aspirate category
  has reappeared, probably through neighboring loans. Therefore modern voiced
  aspiration is not automatically inherited even in Kohistani Shina.
- p. 24: final short unaccented i/e/u/o may become glides following an accented
  vowel; their morphology is why they retain vowel symbols. Word-internal
  redundant glide suffixes are written y/w. Spelling must not be mechanically
  counted as spoken syllables. Earlier four-tone analysis was over-differentiated.
- p. 25: acoustic investigation of 91 items in a carrier frame; short, level,
  falling and rising did not furnish a complete four-way minimal contrast.
- p. 26: all short stressed vowels have high pitch; long vowels permit high
  falling and low rising, with variation in slopes. No short low-pitch contrast
  found. Low onset was more diagnostic than rise. A marginal exceptional ‘hole’
  could reflect initial nasal pitch depression; do not make a new tone category.
- p. 27: short high, long high-falling, long low-rising minimal triple **mos**
  ‘I, agent’, **móos** ‘month’, **moós** ‘flesh’. Word stress among short syllables
  is not predictable. Unstressed long vowels occur, including after suffixation.
- p. 28: unstressed long vowels phonetically half-long and a marginal category;
  authors adopt a mora-accent account with one high accent and low unaccented
  moras. This is their descriptive phonology, not a dated tonogenetic chronology.

## Complete Kohistani grammar: noun and verb evidence

Schmidt & Kohistani 2008, *A Grammar of the Shina Language of Indus Kohistan*,
local research copy `/Users/aryamanarora/Downloads/Shina Language of Indus
Kohistan, A Grammar of the (Schmidt & Kohistani).pdf`, 273 PDF pages. Printed
page = PDF page − 9. Selected-page OCR in `/tmp/palula-accent/kohistani-ocr/` is
an aid to reading, NOT a reliable source for diacritics. Pages 42–50 and 176
checked as images. The book itself is not to be redistributed with this essay.

- p49 automatic accent advancement applies to SECOND-MORA long accents in
  suffixless M3/F3 nouns. Newly unaccented root vowels shorten: caár/caré
  'direction', kaál/kalí 'year'; early góoṣ/góoẓi 'house', núum/núumi 'name',
  yóoṣ/yóoẓa 'mill' remain fixed. Loans do not always follow the generalization.
- p49 fn6 short don 'tooth' → dódi but don 'flock' → doní. The citation form
  cannot predict the class of a short-vowel noun. p47 ǰip/ǰíba 'tongue' fixed,
  versus Palula ǰip/ǰipí mobile, is another important inherited comparison.
- p43 áṣpoo 'horse' has an unaccented long final vowel. Do not generalize the
  Gurezi claim that all long vowels are accented to Kohistani. The footnote's
  OIA áśvaká-/aśvaká- explanation needs independent review: Turner 920 does
  not straightforwardly give the printed accented formation and Vedic label.
- p42 táaro/táara 'star', áẓo/áẓa 'cloud', jamc̣ó/jamc̣á 'son-in-law' show
  that final gender vowels are not uniformly long or of a single derivation.
- p43 hyúu/hyúui 'heart', gugúu/gugwíi 'ankle bone'; p46 ráati/ráatye/ráatyo
  'night'; p47 ac̣híi/achyée 'eye' illustrate later glide formation/contraction
  within inflection. p50 explicitly identifies contour traces of assimilated
  or deleted oblique vowels before vowel-initial case suffixes.
- p176 shortened verbs undergo several vowel-coalescence rules. khyeé 'having
  eaten' and khyáao 'he ate' are probably analogical to pyeé, pyáao 'drink',
  since inherited khā́dati does not independently supply the glide. This is a
  concrete case where analogy is better than an invented sound change.
- p176 and fn38: byoón 'sit' has second-mora accent throughout its perfective
  forms, beé-t-o 'he sat', whereas jaá? [verify: image reads jáa-l-o] 'sprouted'
  and róo-l-o 'cried' have early accent. The authors derive beét- from
  upaviṣṭa- and associate its rising pitch with loss of ṣ in the cluster.
  Palula bhéṭu has short é. This is a second compensatory/cluster-loss case
  alongside Gilgiti taáto, but does NOT license lengthening after every cluster
  reduction (compare short cíṭo < tiktá and páṭo < páttra).
- p135 OCR suggests identical infinitive caroón for 'graze' intransitive and
  transitive, contrasting cáram/carám and cármas/carámas. Check images before
  quoting. Verb classes, aspect suffixes and valency are necessary controls;
  do not compare an OIA finite verb accent mechanically to any modern form.

## Köhnlein: representation is not chronology

The nine-page FASAL paper has been read in full. Its analysis uses an
intonational LH* accent aligned with different foot structures, rather than
two primitive lexical contours. It is explicitly tentative about pragmatics
and position. It also discusses short don/don and competing accounts of noun
classes, pp5–6: do not portray it as overlooking the lexical-class problem.
Its comparison with Lithuanian de Saussure's law is a synchronic analytical
parallel, not proof of a shared historical innovation.
https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/jsal/index.php/fasal/article/view/252/144

## Lorimer 1924: historical disagreement about what was heard

*Notes on the Phonetics of the Gilgit Dialect of Shina*, pp206–208 expresses
skepticism about lexical tone. Lorimer could not reproduce Bailey's judgments
consistently, and heard differences of quantity, aspiration, stress and quality.
He proposed, but did not perform, a listener classification test. pp3–5 limit
the primary observations to Sarfaraz's Amperi speech, checked with Shah Rais;
the paper disclaims uniformity across Gilgit. pp10–11 distinguish long from
ultra-long vowels, including 'meat' and 'hot', which may partly encode the
modern contrast differently, but each lexical comparison needs verification.
Read as cached full PDF via web; original public-domain scan:
https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Indian%20Subcontinent/1924%20Notes%20on%20the%20Phonetics%20of%20the%20Gilgit%20Dialect%20of%20Shina%20by%20Lorimer%20s.pdf

Later acoustic work supports a lexical contrast. Lorimer's negative judgment
does not establish that tone arose in the twentieth century. Equally, it is
not enough to disprove genuine tone loss in a different Shinaic language.

## Further source and chronology checks

- Sharma 1998, *Tribal Languages of Ladakh I, Part One*, 207-page public Pahar
  scan: printed = PDF page minus 18. Printed p33 inspected in the browser. The
  account makes length largely predictable, with contrast principally in i/u;
  pp33–35 do not demonstrate merger of lexical accent. Fieldwork was in 1983
  and 1985, not 1998. The phonology lacks an adequate accentual paradigm section.
  Do not adopt the book's unsupported substrate and archaicity claims. Ramaswami
  reader's CIIL download is a one-page cover; the School Books Archive grammar
  requires requesting a copy. Neither is a full text consulted for this essay.
- Liljegren 2009 pp38–42 explicitly permits a push chain for the Palula vowels.
  Nonfeeding of newly lengthened aa establishes separate old/new vowel outcomes,
  not uniquely a discrete raising-before-lengthening chronology. Biori merges
  old oo and new oo from aa as uu. A second alternative is parallel raising in
  Sawi/Ashret versus restricted independent Biori changes. The post now preserves
  both alternatives instead of using these correspondences as a unique tree.
- Puri in Liljegren 2009 n32 has **bhruú**, late, 'brother', beside **brahú**
  'brothers'. Do not misquote it as early bhrúu. It provides aspiration-position
  evidence, but its relation to other Palula brother accents is not settled.
- Sawi in Knobloch 2020: only a/aː is a pure length opposition. Orthographic ee
  denotes /e/, against e /ɛ/, rather than proving a length contrast. Liljegren's
  i/ii and u/uu mergers are phonemic quantity mergers; phonetic duration varies.
  Sawi beeṭhu retains internal aspiration where Palula bhéṭu has initial
  aspiration. Thus not every aspiration-position difference is deletion.
- Existing Jambu 20260725-ssnp.csv contains exactly 318 Ushojo rows, language
  SSNP-ushojo-USH. The older raw_data/ushojo file uses legacy SIL character codes:

## Sawi: scope of Gomes's negative result

Gomes 2014 pp19–23 read completely: 28/71 basic and 26/71 inflected nouns match
Palula pitch contours, usually where the Palula contour coincides with the
common final-rise pattern in Sawi. 62% of combined observations share that
general pattern. Author concludes no evidence for Palula-like lexical pitch
accent, with an alternative focal mechanism suggested, not established.
The reported z-tests compare proportions to a simple majority, not an explicit
model of chance correspondence under different contour distributions. Do not
present them as a formal reconstruction test or evidence that every mismatch
is a sound change. One speaker, nouns only, one framing regime; other word
classes and a different lexical tone system remain unexcluded (author p21).
Knobloch 2020: voiced aspiration discussion is printed p12; stress explicitly
not investigated p15. Earlier notes giving p10 for aspiration should be corrected.

## Shina nasal backing screen and earlier descriptions

Schmidt/Kaul2008 printed258,259,264,268,271,275 checked as images. Recurrent
short o in dánta→don, kárṇa→koṇ, kárman→kom/krom, cárman→com, páñca→poš and
pánthā→pon; jantú→jon has originally final accent. Nasal environments explain
a shared quality correspondence, not the modern fixed/mobile classes. The r
cluster must simplify/metathesize before a strictly immediate pre-nasal rule.
Contra unrestricted nasal backing: Gilgiti khaṇ, Kohistani khoṇ~khaṇ; pretonic
panzéy fifteen has a but Guresi thumb has initial o. Do not assign a general
accented-only condition without dealing with the latter. Long mā́sa produces
Gilgiti máaz but Kohistani/Guresi/Astori móos, so no general all-Shina aa→oo.

Kogan2020, JLR18(3–4)261–275 (downloaded public publisher PDF), pp263–264
reconstructs *a→o before final nasals in Indo-Iranian loans in Tibetan, with
blocking by consonant clusters and following historical i. This is an account
of a proposed donor lect, not a demonstrated Shina sound law; it cannot simply
be transferred to the present data or date Shina backing to pre-Tibetan times.
His note11 explicitly distinguishes nasal conditioning from Kashmiri u-umlaut.
No new phylogenetic or chronological claim is adopted from that comparison.

Morgenstierne1941p11 page image checked: long vowels tend to be circumflexed;
stress usually last heavy syllable no farther back than antepenult, but with
exceptions; he explicitly did not notice lexically/morphologically relevant
tone. Pp13–14 already describe much dialectal vowel differentiation. This
is a caution about descriptive completeness, not evidence that modern Palula
lexical tone arose entirely after his fieldwork. P15 suggests r-dissimilation
*rātlī in night; that is an existing segmental proposal, not a solution to the
modern raát/róot accent and inflection difference.
