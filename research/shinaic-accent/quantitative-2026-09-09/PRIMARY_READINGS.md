## Liljegren 2013, Notes on Kalkoti, pp. 140–144

Read the complete tonal analysis, F0 discussion, and Tables 12–13. The study recorded 250 items from three speakers both in isolation and in a constant utterance frame. It presents the extra contrasts beyond high/default versus low as tentative and notes the lack of minimal pairs for several categories.

Table 12's phonetic groups are essential even when a segmental DB citation lacks an acute: high-level/default (lake, path, ear, cow, blood, tree, tooth, head), low-level (lip, wheat, left, grass, night, belly, sister, snow), low-rising with glottalized isolation form (horse, paternal aunt, goat, field, far), high-rising with glottalized isolation form (long, star, hair, paternal grandfather, paternal grandmother, husband's brother, knee, grandson), high-falling (wood, root, son). Some low phonetic pitch with voiceless aspiration is predictable rather than necessarily lexical.

Table 13 analyzes wood's falling contour as Low linked to the second mora; Hultman 2023 instead uses early High for šáak. Keep source theory separate from observed contour. Do not infer a 2013→2023 diachronic reversal from the symbols alone. Liljegren proposes inherited accent becoming default stress, onset aspiration producing Low, final h/aspiration producing late Low, and loss of a final unaccented vowel producing a glottal segment and then final High. These are hypotheses with explicitly acknowledged unresolved cases, not established exceptionless laws. Footnote 15 gives pitri (0), ḍä̀rin (L), ic̣ì (L), lumaáṭ (H), bä̀kaál (LH).

Important provenance correction: `dialect:Kalk:HKAT-xka:...` on a merged record does not identify its bibliographic source. Records with `source_keys=kalkoti` are Liljegren 2013. HKAT source is `liljegren-hindukush`. Source tags and dialect labels must be read independently.

## Hultman 2023, Topics in the grammar of Kalkoti, pp. 14–20

Read the complete tone discussion and Tables 9–10. Hultman distinguishes early/late High and syllable-wide Low; multiple Highs can occur in longer words. He explicitly warns that many unmarked tokens have uncertain tone, especially older recordings, while marked H/L is more secure. Only explicitly analyzed toneless examples may be coded as zero tone.

Table 9 contrasts toneless tooth, blood, four, seed, head; early-H wood, boy, song; late-H star, paternal grandmother, mother, long. The latter group generally corresponds to Palula words with a final unaccented vowel; meéš ‘man’ is a counterexample. Table 10 compares low-tone Kalkoti with Palula voiced aspiration/h, including becomes, ground, belly, liver, took, salt, comes, laughed, far, horse. Meat/near/song do not follow a naive Palula sonorant-aspiration→Low rule. Night's Low is not explained by Palula raát and may involve Gawri contact. Predictable lowering with retained voiceless aspiration is distinguished from lexical Low.

## Liljegren 2016, A grammar of Palula, pp. 112–123

Read the nominal declension history. The a-declension distinguishes mobile deés/deesá, ghoóṣṭ/ghooṣṭá, putr/putrá, ṣiṣ/ṣiṣá from fixed patterns. The author explicitly marks village díiš as an accent/raising irregularity. New short-a lengthening must be separated from inherited long-vowel raising.

The proposed -aka > -akō > -agō > -ahu > -au > -o/u history is accompanied by analogical expansion, so an unaccented final u does not independently prove historical -aka. Heart híṛu may reach the same class from -aya. Feminine m-declension i nouns include both plausible -ikā derivatives and convergent forms such as duck héeṛi and thigh séeti.

Pages 122–123 reconstruct egg haṇoó and son-in-law ǰhamatroó through earlier final early áa > óo followed by later accent advancement to oó. Boy phoó has plural phaayá; brother bhroó has plural bhraawú. These paradigms provide more evidence for an earlier aa stage than the isolated modern oo alone. The broad Turner boy family also contains old ō variants, so the proposed route must still be distinguished from alternatives.
