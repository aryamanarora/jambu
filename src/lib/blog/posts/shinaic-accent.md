Shinaic words preserve more history than their consonants and vowels alone reveal. The position of an accent can distinguish words, distinguish grammatical forms, and help explain why a vowel changed its quality. But the tempting statement that Palula simply preserves Sanskrit accent is too simple. Some accents survive; some move when a vowel disappears; and some rising accents are demonstrably younger than the words in which they occur.

This essay asks how few changes we need to explain those layers. The main evidence comes from [Liljegren's grammar](https://doi.org/10.17169/langsci.b82.85), the [Palula dictionary](ref:liljegren), and the comparative study of [Palula, Sawi, and Kalkoti](https://doi.org/10.5617/ao.5341). Ashret, or Southern Palula, is the default variety below. Biori, or Northern Palula, supplies a crucial comparison: several changes that affected closed syllables in Ashret did not operate there.

The central result is a layered history: inherited lexical accent survives in conservative formations; vowel loss and contraction alter where it can be realized; Palula's accent-sensitive vowel changes and Kalkoti's new high and low tones add younger contrasts. Morphological remodeling accounts for another recurring pattern, but cannot be used to dismiss every difficult word. The [compact account](#the-smallest-account-the-evidence-supports) and [unresolved cases](#the-remaining-counterexamples-are-part-of-the-result) are collected below; the [evidence package](../research/shinaic-accent/shinaic-accent-evidence.zip) makes the comparisons and their limits inspectable.

## The comparison has to include the whole family

Here **Shinaic** means Shina and its regional varieties, including Kohistani Shina, together with Palula, Sawi (Sauji), Kalkoti, Kundal Shahi, Ushojo, and Brokskat. **Indus Kohistani is a comparative neighbour, distinct from Kohistani Shina**. A similar sounding name or a similar tone system does not establish membership in the group. The internal branching also remains a hypothesis to test, rather than something an accent reconstruction can simply assume.

Three different questions must be kept apart: where an earlier word was accented; what happened to the vowel that carried that accent; and how the modern language realizes the resulting contrast. These questions can have different answers in closely related languages. Palula's two positions for accent on a long vowel cannot simply be copied into the analysis of Kalkoti's more complex system. Nor does an older transcription without tone marks establish that a language lacked tone.

The evidence is uneven. Palula has an accent-marked dictionary and detailed inflectional paradigms. Shina has substantial descriptions and comparative lists, but its regional varieties cannot be represented by a single undifferentiated “Shina” form. Kalkoti has two recent acoustic analyses. Kundal Shahi has a preliminary description with revealing paradigms. Ushojo, Sawi, and Brokskat present different gaps, which matter to the conclusions.

Within Northern Palula, the small amount of evidence from **Puri**, in Shishi Koh, also deserves to remain distinct. Liljegren reports **bhruú** ‘brother’ beside **brahú** ‘brothers’, and **gháw** ‘cow’ beside **gehí** ‘cows’. The position of aspiration varies with the paradigm. This supports a historical relation between aspiration and accent placement, but not the claim that aspiration created every lexical accent. Nor should Puri's late **bhruú** be silently equated with an early-accented dictionary form. [Liljegren 2009: 9, 35 n. 32](https://doi.org/10.5617/ao.5341).

## What must be explained

The acute accent marks the high part of a word. A short vowel has one timing unit, or *mora*; a long vowel has two. In the transcription used here, **áa** has accent on the first mora and a falling contour, while **aá** has accent on the second and a rising contour. A monosyllable with a short vowel is generally written without an acute because no contrast between its two halves is possible. This does not mean it is accentless. The notation follows Liljegren (2016: 72–75).

Accent belongs to a word's lexical and grammatical structure. It cannot be predicted just by looking for an aspirated consonant. The grammar documents breathiness before both accented and unaccented vowels, and before both early and late accent (2016: 69–72). Aspiration is important to some later developments, but cannot by itself explain the origin of the entire accent system.

Gurezi provides a direct test: **phaál** ‘ploughshare’ and **pháal** ‘initiate a marriage proposal’ have the same aspirated onset and different contours in Ahmed's field study. Gilgiti supplies another caution. Hussain's experiment on five speakers found that **voiceless aspirated stops raised the following vowel's fundamental frequency** relative to the other stop categories. That experiment used controlled nonsense syllables; it did not test the origin of lexical accent. A consonant's immediate effect on pitch and an inherited lexical contrast are different kinds of evidence. [Ahmed 2016: 103–104](https://real.mtak.hu/35708/1/062.2016.69.1.6.pdf), [Hussain 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2226-471X/6/3/139).

The historical descriptions require similar care. Bailey explicitly recognized meaningful tone contrasts, whereas Lorimer was skeptical of some judgments and proposed a listener test. These were both published in 1924, although Bailey's preface dates to 1917. Their disagreement does not date tonogenesis to the twentieth century. Later work establishes the modern contrasts more securely; the older transcriptions remain useful when their individual conventions are checked. [Bailey 1924: 4–6, 84](https://pahar.in/indian-subcontinent-books/), [Lorimer 1924: 206–208](https://pahar.in/indian-subcontinent-books/).

Palula has its own cautionary example. Morgenstierne noticed circumflexed long vowels and described a tendency to stress the last heavy syllable, with qualifications. Yet he reported no lexically or morphologically relevant tone. His account already records much of the dialectal vowel differentiation discussed below. Its failure to identify a contrast that later investigators document securely is evidence about the limits of the earlier description; it does not establish that Palula acquired its entire accent system between the two studies. [Morgenstierne 1941: 11, 13–14](https://mahraka.com/pdf/Notes%20on%20Phalura.pdf).

There is a distinction between reconstructing **accent placement** and reconstructing its phonetic realization. Continuity of a lexical contrast does not establish that today's rising and falling contours were pronounced the same way in Vedic times. Nor does it settle the best formal analysis of modern Shina. Köhnlein proposes that the two contours result from different rhythmic structures and different alignment of the same intonational pitch movement, rather than two independently stored tone melodies. His analysis draws support from accent advancement in inflection, while explicitly treating the proposed pitch alignment as tentative. Either representation must explain the historical word and paradigm correspondences. [Köhnlein 2022: 2–6](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/jsal/index.php/fasal/article/view/252/144).

A complete replacement of old accent by predictable weight-based stress is therefore a real competing historical hypothesis, but it carries a burden of explanation. Guresi **báal** ‘hair’ and **baál** ‘boy’ correspond to **vā́la-** and **bālá-**: the old long vowel has the same quantity and syllabic position in both comparisons, while its relation to the accent differs. A rule based only on those vowel weights would merge them. One could seek different intervening derivatives or other lost conditioning, and such histories demonstrably matter in some words below. To replace inherited lexical accent throughout the family, however, those conditions would need independent evidence across the cognate sets, rather than being reconstructed solely to recover each observed contour. The retention account is economical because it predicts a recurring distinction before these additional repairs. [Basu 2025, abstract](https://researchmap.jp/sakuraishikawa/presentations/51401293/attachment_file.pdf), [Schmidt and Kaul 2008](https://www.wisdomlib.org/uploads/journals/acta-orie/vol-69-2008/7372-6759-23258.pdf).

## A lost ending can leave its accent behind

The clearest historical account starts with nouns whose old final vowel was accented. Compare the following source analyses, with asterisks marking intermediate reconstructions rather than attested words:

| Old Indo-Aryan | Reconstructed intermediate | Ashret singular | Inflected form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *divasá-* ‘day’ | **\*deesá** | [**deés**](form:f_puvr4yqeajsj4) | **deesá** |
| *gōṣṭhá-* ‘cow-house’ | **\*ghooṣṭá** | [**ghoóṣṭ**](form:f_wnpvd2nljzuuk) ‘house’ | **ghooṣṭá** |
| *putrá-* ‘son’ | **\*putrá** | [**putr**](form:f_z2ntq7ceeuxyo) | **putrá** |

Deleting the final accented vowel puts the accent on the last surviving vowel mora. Where a longer ending survives in the rest of the paradigm, its accent remains there. The resulting alternation looks like accent moving to a suffix whenever we inflect the modern word, but a historical explanation can derive it from the loss of an ending in only part of the paradigm. This is Liljegren's account (2016: 112–114); the exact intervening Middle Indo-Aryan forms still require reconstruction.

The short vowel in **putr** is especially useful. It cannot display a first-mora/second-mora contrast, yet its inflection exposes the same historical division. Looking only at the citation forms would throw away evidence.

There is also real counterevidence. **Díiš** ‘village’ has fixed stem accent, although its proposed ancestor **dēśá-** has final accent. Liljegren explicitly identifies the problem (2016: 113). Calling it an exception records the failure; it does not explain it. A satisfactory account must check the source accent, alternative formations, possible borrowing, and analogy before proposing another sound law.

## Did loans introduce Palula's late accent?

Richard Strand proposes a substantially different origin for the late contour. Among 233 Palula words with accented long vowels and accented Old Indo-Aryan comparisons, he counts 37 with old noninitial accent and modern late accent, but 63 with old noninitial accent and modern early accent. The remaining words have old initial accent: 129 are now early and four late. In another sample, at least 180 of 283 words with final late accent are loans. He therefore suggests that pronouncing final-accented loans with the late contour established a habit which spread to native suffixes. This concerns the origin of the late contour; he does not propose that Palula lost all continuity with the older accent system. [Strand 2016 preprint: 22, Table 28](https://nuristan.info/PDFs/Strand%20-%20Phonatory%20Location-2016-06-07.pdf).

The contrary evidence is substantial, but the counts do not yet decide between the histories. **37/233 is the share of the entire selected sample**, while **37/100** is the late-contour share among its old noninitial forms. Neither denominator supplies a historical success rate without separating the inputs. An inherited short vowel lengthened later, an old long vowel, and a newly suffixed adjective need not retain the old accent in the same way. The 63 early outcomes are therefore a cohort to explain, rather than 63 interchangeable counterexamples. The same caution applies to the 129 early outcomes: they cannot all be counted as independent proof of uninterrupted inheritance.

The present lexical screen illustrates the issue without replicating Strand's sample. Among its 187 nominal, adjectival and numeral records, **46** pair a noninitially accented source label with a modern early-accented long vowel; **27** of those carry the formation-control flag. That flag means that the formation needs investigation, not that the discrepancy has been explained. The remaining records include both predicted outcomes such as **saptá → sáat** and unresolved ones such as **dēśá → díiš**. A raw count treats these very different histories alike.

The loan count establishes that late accent is common in borrowed vocabulary. It does not establish the direction of influence: loans can enter a pre-existing accent class. To argue that they created the contrast, we need identifiable borrowing periods and matched inherited paradigms that show how and when the new pattern spread. The related Shina classes also need an explanation. I therefore retain **loan-driven reinforcement or extension** as a plausible mechanism, without making it the sole origin of late accent.

The disagreement also reaches the transcriptions. Strand's phonetic examples give **pʹûtr/pʹûtra** ‘son/sons’, with long early-accented **uu** and fixed accent, against Liljegren's short, mobile **putr/putrá**. He also records late **ghâʹnu** ‘big’, against **gháanu**. These must remain source-specific observations until speaker, variety, and recording differences are resolved. They weaken any claim that one modern dictionary exhausts Palula's accentual variation. [Strand, phonology, “Phonation and Laryngeal Accent” and Figures 2, 9](https://nuristan.info/IndoAryan/Indus/Atsaret/AtsaretLanguage/Lexicon/phon.html).

## Vowel changes reveal a later layer

Biori **kram**, with inflected **kráama**, corresponds to Ashret **kráam**, **kráama** ‘work’. The economical explanation is that accented short *a* first lengthened in open syllables, and later lengthened in closed syllables in Ashret as well. This ordering accounts for a difference both between dialects and within a single paradigm (Liljegren 2009: 23–24; 2016: 78–80).

That later lengthening did not always produce the same accent. In Ashret closed syllables, **báaṭ** ‘stone’ has early accent, while **khaár** ‘donkey’ and **basaánd** ‘spring’ have late accent. Liljegren's proposed conditioning is aspiration in the onset, including *h*, or an earlier unaccented syllable in the word (2009: 21). A particularly revealing example is **haát** ‘hand’ beside **háata** ‘hands’: the different outcomes fit lengthening at different stages and in different syllable structures.

This gives a warning for historical reconstruction. A modern rising accent need not continue an old accent on a lost ending. A later vowel change can produce the same surface result. Conversely, a falling accent need not imply a historically fixed paradigm: **báaṭ** inflects as **baṭá**. The modern vowel contour and the noun's accentual class are two different pieces of evidence.

The corresponding changes in vowel quality let us order events more closely:

| Environment | Earlier vowel | Ashret outcome | Biori comparison |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Earlier accented open syllable | short **a** | **áa** | **áa** |
| Earlier accented open syllable | early-accented **áa** | **óo** | **úu** |
| Earlier accented open syllable | early-accented **ée**, **óo** | **íi**, **úu** | **íi**, **úu** |
| Later accented closed syllable | short **a** | **áa** or **aá**, with the conditioning above | short **a** retained |
| Later accented closed syllable | early-accented **áa**, **ée** | **óo**, **íi** | **aa**, **ee** retained |

The first three rows describe a chain shift, not instructions to feed every newly created vowel through the next row. Ashret keeps the outcomes of old **a**, **aa**, and **oo** distinct as **aa**, **oo**, and **uu**. A serial derivation can achieve this by applying the raising rules before the lengthening that supplies fresh **aa**. But that is an ordering of rules in an analysis, not proof that the historical shifts happened in discrete stages: Liljegren also considers a push chain in which the moving vowels displace one another. Biori differs precisely because both older **oo** and the new **oo** from **aa** end up as **uu**. Its additional closed-syllable **óo → úu** affects early accent; late **oó** is retained. [Liljegren 2009: 20–24, 38–42](https://doi.org/10.5617/ao.5341).

There is a competing account of the branches, too. An early shift in open syllables shared by the two Palula varieties, followed by an Ashret extension into closed syllables, fits their internal paradigms well. When Sawi enters the comparison, Liljegren also considers parallel histories: a more general raising chain in Sawi and Ashret, alongside independently restricted changes in Biori. The vowel correspondences establish the conditioning and the old/new vowel distinctions more firmly than they establish a unique family tree. I use the two-stage Palula derivation as an economical working model, without treating every apparently shared raising as a proven common innovation.

Even the label “open syllable” needs a qualification when the account is extended beyond **kram/kráama**. Biori **dan → dáanda** ‘tooth’ has long **aa** before an internal nasal–stop cluster, and the grammar describes syllable boundaries through comparable internal clusters. Biori **náang → núunga** ‘nail’ likewise raises the vowel before **ng** when an ending is added. These facts cannot simply be omitted from an exceptionless rule formulated over modern open syllables. Earlier cluster syllabification could differ, or the vowel alternation could have spread through the inflectional class, on the model of transparent **kaṇ/káaṇa** and **kram/kráama**. The latter is an identifiable analogical model, although its historical operation is not independently demonstrated. I retain open-syllable change as the core reconstruction and leave its extension to internal nasal clusters unsettled. [Liljegren 2016: 67–68, 80](https://doi.org/10.17169/langsci.b82.85).

The same Biori table gives **baazáar → baazúura** ‘bazaar’. A loan can participate in the inherited-looking alternation. This warns against dating every modern instance to the original chain shift: an alternation created by a sound change can become part of an inflectional pattern and extend to newer words. It also prevents the sound-change list from replacing a description of the modern morphology.

Accent is part of the conditioning. Ashret raises earlier **first-mora** long vowels, while late-accented long vowels can retain their quality. This is why **díiš** ‘village’ is more informative than an unexplained accent mark alone: the vowel **ii** supports an earlier accented **ee** stage. The retraction required by its proposed **dēśá-** etymology would have to precede that raising. It cannot be an arbitrarily recent repair.

Umlaut adds a separate, partly productive change: **aa → ee** before **i** in the next syllable. Thus **táatu → téeti** ‘hot’, **gháanu → ghéeni** ‘big’, and **paṇáaru → paṇéeri** ‘white’ alternate by gender. The same fronting operates in later vocabulary: **kitaáb → kiteebí** ‘book’ and **gilaás → gileesí** ‘glass’. These examples show that umlaut cannot be confined to a single prehistoric moment. Its outputs must also be kept distinct from older **ee** that participated in raising. Morphological analogy can extend an old alternation after the sound change that originally created it has ceased to operate. [Liljegren 2016: 80–83](https://doi.org/10.17169/langsci.b82.85).

Strand supplies a more specific ordering argument. His **dʹôdo** ‘paternal grandfather’ and **dʹêdi** ‘paternal grandmother’ permit **\*dáado → dóodo**, beside **\*dáadi → déedi**. In a serial account, **ée → íi** must precede the umlaut that creates this **ee**; otherwise the feminine should have **ii**. Umlaut probably precedes **áa → óo**, because the reverse order would require fronting **oo** as well. He explicitly acknowledges exceptions and repeated applications in other formations. This supports an **older umlaut layer within the vowel chronology**, alongside the later productive extensions. It does not establish a single date for every fronted vowel. [Strand, phonology, “Vocalic Processes within the Word,” note 1](https://nuristan.info/IndoAryan/Indus/Atsaret/AtsaretLanguage/Lexicon/phon.html).

The dictionary audit places a useful limit on the closed-syllable rule. There are 36 noun or adjective entries where a closed, accented **aa** citation form can be matched literally to a short-**a** morphemic stem. Thirty-five have the predicted accent; **dhráac̣** ‘grape’ does not. Its proposed ancestor **drā́kṣā-** already had long **ā**, but Biori **dhrac̣** has a short vowel. We would have to establish the intervening shortening and aspiration history before deciding which rule should apply. Neither its Sanskrit length nor its modern short alternant can settle that alone. This is a descriptive check, not a 35-out-of-36 historical success rate. Some other entries have old long vowels, uncertain etymologies, or borrowed stems. Conversely, **kháaṇ** ‘mountain’ must remain in view even though the dictionary does not supply the short morphemic form that would admit it to that restricted sample. Its early accent is a real difficulty for the proposed late-lengthening rule under either **khaṇḍá-** or **skandhá-**.

The same ordering explains why a numeral can disagree with the Sanskrit accent without requiring a special numeral sound law. **saptá- → \*sat → sáat** ‘seven’ transfers the accent to a short vowel, then lengthens it with early accent. **Áaṣṭ** ‘eight’ permits the same kind of derivation from **aṣṭā́**. These forms do not by themselves establish accentual analogy among the numerals. The contracted compounds ‘eleven’ through ‘nineteen’ present a separate and harder problem.

The teens nevertheless have an internal regularity worth recovering. The dictionary pairs **akóoš, bóoš, satóoš, aṣṭóoš** ‘11, 12, 17, 18’ with morphemic stems in **-áaš**, and **tríiš, čandíiš, panǰíiš, ṣoṛíiš** ‘13–16’ with stems in **-éeš**. The vowel changes are the familiar **áa → óo**, **ée → íi**, once the contracted and accented stems exist. ‘Twelve’ and ‘eighteen’ have useful old models in **dvā́daśa** and **aṣṭā́daśa**: the accent already falls on the vowel that precedes **daśa**. ‘Eleven’, ‘fourteen’, ‘fifteen’, and ‘sixteen’ do not simply preserve the accents of **ḗkādaśa**, **cáturdaśa**, **páñcadaśa**, and **ṣṓḍaśa**.

A testable proposal is **analogical accent on the contracted teen ending**, with the already compatible ‘twelve’ and ‘eighteen’ among the models. That would explain several accent discrepancies together, before the vowels acquired their present raised qualities. It is not yet a complete derivation: the spread of **ee** into ‘fourteen’ through ‘sixteen’, the consonants of **čandíiš**, and the exact contracted forms still need reconstruction. Turner's alternative **\*trayēdaśa** for ‘thirteen’ does not independently attest the accent assigned to it in the Palula dictionary. Shina **akáai** ‘eleven’ and Gilgiti **panzéy** ‘fifteen’ suggest that some restructuring may precede the specifically Palula vowel shifts. This is an identifiable analogical hypothesis, not a new exceptionless sound law. [Palula dictionary, numeral entries](ref:liljegren), [Liljegren 2016: 162–164](https://doi.org/10.17169/langsci.b82.85), [Schmidt and Kaul 2008: 275](https://www.wisdomlib.org/uploads/journals/acta-orie/vol-69-2008/7372-6759-23258.pdf).

Verbs provide a much larger warning against overgeneralization. All **34 explicitly supplied imperatives containing accented aa** in the dictionary have early accent, including **dháar**, **háar**, **čháaḍ**, **biṣáam**, and **utráap**. Aspiration and a preceding syllable do not give them the late accent predicted by the noun pattern. The grammar independently describes imperative formation with **á → áa**, alongside **áa → óo** and **ée → íi**. This is a regularity within the verbal paradigm, not 34 unrelated phonetic exceptions. A historical account may derive its uniformity through leveling from other accented stem forms, but that further step remains an inference. [Liljegren 2016: 226](https://doi.org/10.17169/langsci.b82.85).

## Word formation can replace an inherited accent

Many Palula and Shina adjectives end in a gender vowel and accent the final syllable of the preceding root. This pattern cuts across the accent of the Old Indo-Aryan comparison:

| Old Indo-Aryan comparison | Palula | Other Shina evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **kṛṣṇá-** ‘black’ | **kriṣíṇ-u** | Gilgiti **kíṇ-o**, Guresi **kíṇ-u** |
| **dákṣiṇa-** ‘right’ | **deec̣híṇ-u** | Kohistani **dac̣híṇ-o**, Guresi **dac̣íṇ-u** |
| **pā́ṇḍara-** ‘white’ | **paṇáar-u** | The Palula paradigm supplies the test here |
| **śītalá-** ‘cold’ | **šidáal-u** | Gilgiti **šidál-o** |

The recurring target is more economical than separate accent changes for ‘black’, ‘right’, ‘white’, and ‘cold’. The consonants determine how many root syllables survive: Palula inserts a vowel within the difficult consonant sequence in ‘black’, while main Shina has a shorter root. The accentual pattern can remain the same relative to the resulting root even when the word's syllable count differs.

This supports **formation-specific accent assignment before the gender ending**. It does not establish that every final **-u**, **-o**, or **-i** directly continues the same suffix. Old **-aka-** and **-ikā-** extensions are relevant to many nouns and adjectives, but kinship formations, older stem vowels, and later remodeling must be distinguished. The generalization is also about the accented *syllable*, not automatically its first mora: contracted forms such as **lhoóku** ‘small’ still require their own vowel history. Basu's 2025 conference abstract explicitly proposes this distinction between inherited accent in unextended stems and root-final accent in extended stems. The pattern should be credited to that proposal; the comparisons here help test its scope, while the abstract alone does not demonstrate every derivation. [Basu 2025, abstract](https://researchmap.jp/sakuraishikawa/presentations/51401293/attachment_file.pdf), [Schmidt and Kaul 2008](https://www.wisdomlib.org/uploads/journals/acta-orie/vol-69-2008/7372-6759-23258.pdf).

Basu also proposes attraction from an accented short vowel to a following long vowel, illustrated with Indus Kohistani. The Shinaic comparison supplies a candidate case: Palula **angóor** and Shina **agáar/aṅgáaru** ‘fire’ place their accent on the old long vowel of **áṅgāra-**. The agreement argues against a Palula-only accident. Yet a long vowel that merely happens to be accented in the modern language cannot prove this rule: independent evidence must establish both its earlier length and the earlier accent. Nor can weight attraction replace the morphological rule in the preceding table, where the formations and their final vowels matter.

The wider Palula screen gives **karáaṛu** ‘leopard’, compared with **káḍāra-**, as another possible attraction case. It is less decisive because root-final accent in an extended noun predicts the same result. There are also apparent counterexamples: **páand** ‘path’ from **pánthā-**, and **sígal** ‘sand’ from **síkatā-**, retain initial prominence despite the old final long vowel. Deletion of those final vowels *before* attraction would remove their ability to attract accent, but that ordering is a hypothesis requiring evidence from the intervening formations. These examples keep the rule restricted: one cannot apply “accent the following long vowel” directly to every Sanskrit headword and call the mismatches analogical.

## Shina: the consonant history makes a difference

The familiar Shina contrast **móos** ‘month’ versus **moós** ‘flesh’ has a straightforward historical motivation: compare Old Indo-Aryan **mā́sa-** and **māṃsá-**. An accent originally on the surviving stem and one originally on the lost ending need not have merged when the words became monosyllabic. Kohistani Shina adds short **mos** ‘I, agent case’ to make a three-way contrast of length and accent. Its grammar describes the contrast on the basis of acoustic measurements, while also warning that the exact pitch slopes vary. [Schmidt and Kohistani 2008: 25–28](https://books.google.com/books?id=mZzD86umtbAC&pg=PA25).

This is strong evidence for continuity of an old lexical distinction. It is not evidence that every modern rising vowel arose by apocope. **Gilgiti taáto** ‘hot’ retains a final vowel. Its Old Indo-Aryan comparison is **taptá-**; the intervening cluster is essential. Here is the correspondence in Jambu's existing transcription of Schmidt and Kaul's comparative list:

| Variety | ‘Hot’ | What needs explaining |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gilgiti Shina | **taáto** | Long vowel with late accent |
| Kohistani Shina | **táto** | Short accented vowel |
| Guresi Shina | **tátu** | Short accented vowel |
| Astori Shina | **tátto** | Short vowel before a retained geminate |
| Ashret Palula | **táatu** | Long vowel with early accent |

Schmidt and Kaul explicitly associate the Gilgiti rising vowel with secondary lengthening after cluster reduction, giving **taptá- → \*tatta → taáto** (2008: 244–245, note 17). The full comparison suggests a more precise, testable account. Starting from a common accented stem **\*tátt-**, Astori retains the geminate; Kohistani and Guresi simplify it without preserving its duration as vowel length; Gilgiti compensates with a long vowel whose accent is late; and Palula simplifies the consonant before its independent open-syllable lengthening, which produces early accent. The earlier shift from **taptá-** to a root-accented formation still needs the morphological explanation discussed above. The consonant changes do not do that work by themselves.

The difference between **compensatory lengthening** and **later lengthening in an open syllable** is not just terminology. They make different predictions for accent. It would be uneconomical to call the modern forms four unrelated exceptions when one older cluster and different branch-specific treatments explain their shapes. But it would also be premature to establish the full chronology from ‘hot’ alone. The same predictions must hold in other inherited cluster families.

Kohistani **beéto** ‘he sat’ supplies another relevant case. Its grammar connects the late-accented perfective stem with **upaviṣṭa-**, specifically the loss of **ṣ** in the old cluster. But **tiktá- → cíṭo** ‘bitter’ and **páttra- → páṭho** ‘leaf’ have short vowels in Kohistani, and ‘hot’ itself is short there. The defensible rule is therefore **compensatory lengthening in particular cluster histories**, not “every lost consonant adds a late-accented mora.” The evidence still has to establish which clusters, stages, and dialects participate. [Schmidt and Kohistani 2008: 176, note 38](https://books.google.com/books?id=mZzD86umtbAC&pg=PA176).

Two further controls prevent an easy generalization. Gilgiti **sat** ‘seven’ continues the same old **pt** cluster as **taáto** ‘hot’, but has no long vowel. Loss of the final vowel could have changed the environment for cluster reduction. Yet the comparison cannot simply require short vowels in every final stem: Kohistani and Astori **baát** ‘cooked rice’ are long. Schmidt and Kaul connect that family with **bhaktá-**, and report Buddruss's earlier proposal that short Gilgiti **bat** was borrowed from Kashmiri. That proposal was made before he knew the long Astori form, giving the comparison some independent predictive value. It still requires a borrowing history; it does not license excluding every short vowel as a loan. [Schmidt and Kaul 2008: 244–245 n. 17, 271, 277, 282](https://www.wisdomlib.org/uploads/journals/acta-orie/vol-69-2008/7372-6759-23258.pdf).

The same note derives Gilgiti **leéc̣** ‘sickle’ from **lavítra-**, through a reconstructed short-vowel **letra**. Kohistani **léec̣i** instead has early accent. This is a second published example for the Gilgiti compensation account, but also a reminder that contraction of **avi**, reduction of **tr**, and the surviving feminine ending are separate variables. The short intermediate must be justified before the modern length can be assigned uniquely to cluster loss. The useful generalization is that consonant reduction can create a new long vowel with a different accent from an old long vowel; its complete conditioning remains to be reconstructed.

## Contraction after h loss: the dialects preserve intermediate stages

Radloff's broader survey covers northern Shina varieties around Gilgit, Hunza and Punial; eastern varieties around Astor and Baltistan; the Diamer varieties of Chilas, Darel, Tangir, Harban and Sazin; and Kohistan's Jalkot, Palas and Kolai. Its geographical clusters come from lexical similarity, intelligibility and speakers' judgments. They are useful sampling units, not automatically branches of a reconstructed family tree.

One correspondence is especially relevant to accent. Forms for ‘broom’, ‘light’, and ‘red’ often retain an intervocalic **h** in Diamer and Kohistan, while northern and eastern forms have a contracted long vowel with rising pitch. For ‘light’, the survey compares forms of the type **laˈhoku** with **loóko**; for ‘red’, **liˈhilo/luˈhilo** with **loólo/loólyo**. Here **ˈ** marks syllable stress in the survey's transcription, while the acute on the second vowel marks the rise.

These correspondences support **h loss followed by coalescence**, with a later accented vowel contributing the late peak of the contracted vowel. They cannot all be explained by loss of a word-final accented vowel: the changes occur inside words that retain an ending. Palula **lhoílu** ‘red’ preserves a different arrangement of aspiration and adjoining vowels, consistent with another route through the same kind of contraction problem.

The geographical pattern has exceptions. Gupis, in the northern cluster, retains **h** in the survey's ‘light’ and ‘red’; Diamer contains contracted forms too. Some southern words elsewhere in the list have marked tone. The survey itself emphasizes these qualifications, and calls its transcriptions field records that have not undergone thorough phonological analysis. Thus the comparison supports a local sound change and a route to rising vowels, but not a claim that southern Shina lacks tone or that one neat boundary separates every reflex. [Radloff 1992: 146–148, 207–209, in *Languages of Northern Areas*](ref:backstrom1992).

## The segmental changes that an accent history needs

An accent reconstruction has to use the syllables that existed when a rule applied. Comparing accents by their syllable number in the modern spelling would misalign many of the examples above. The following changes supply the necessary intervening structures.

**Clusters simplify, move, or acquire a vowel.** Palula retains many initial consonant-plus-**r** clusters; Shina varieties often turn them into retroflex consonants. Thus the comparisons for ‘brother’ and ‘long’ involve Palula **bhr-**, **dhr-** beside Shina **ẓ-/ǰ̣-**. Kalkoti instead has **dr-** in ‘brother’ and ‘village’, and **tr-** in ‘work’. Sawi turns old **tr** into a lateral fricative **ɬ**, including **ɬo** ‘three’. Internal **r** can move leftward, as in the history of Palula **kráam** ‘work’; other clusters receive a vowel, as in **kriṣíṇu** ‘black’. These different operations change the number and weight of syllables before accent-conditioned vowel changes apply. They do not constitute one general accent shift. [Liljegren 2009: 34–37](https://doi.org/10.5617/ao.5341), [Schmidt and Kaul 2008: 236–240](https://www.wisdomlib.org/uploads/journals/acta-orie/vol-69-2008/7372-6759-23258.pdf).

**Old sibilant clusters must remain distinct.** Halfmann argues that old **sth** developed into an aspirated stop, dental initially but retroflex internally, while **ṣṭ(h)** could remain a cluster in Shinaic. His Gilgiti **ã́ṭi** ‘bone’, from an extended **asthi-ka-**, therefore need not come from the lexicographers’ **aṣṭhi-** or from an otherwise unidentified donor language. The loss of aspiration and development of nasality still belong to later stages. This improves the consonant history without independently settling the accent of the extended formation. [Halfmann 2024: 203–206](https://doi.org/10.30687/bhasha/2785-5953/2024/02/002).

Palula instead has **haáḍ/háaḍa**, Biori **haḍ/háaḍa**, in the separate **haḍḍa-** family. These fit the Ashret closed-syllable lengthening comparison; they are not evidence for the same consonant derivation as Gilgiti ‘bone’. A shared meaning alone does not make words suitable accentual comparanda. [Palula dictionary, s.v. *haáḍ*](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.5526477).

**Nasal clusters have their own histories.** Palula **dáand** ‘tooth’, **basaánd** ‘spring’, and **yáandr** ‘mill’ include voiced stops in clusters whose comparisons have old **nt**. In Shina, loss of a stop or nasal in a cluster is common, but neither deletion consistently lengthens the vowel nor lengthening consistently produces the same accent. The different reflexes of ‘hot’ make that restriction unavoidable. Shortening also matters: a short modern vowel can continue an older long vowel, so Sanskrit quantity cannot be copied directly into the most recent Palula lengthening rule.

The Shina comparisons also suggest a restricted backing of **a → o** in nasal environments. **dánta- → don**, **kárṇa- → koṇ**, **kárman- → kom/krom**, **cárman- → com**, **páñca → poš**, and **pánthā- → pon** recur across the main Shina varieties. Gilgiti, Guresi, Astori and Drasi **jon** ‘snake’ adds the old finally accented **jantú-**. The change in vowel quality therefore does not itself explain a fixed versus mobile accent class. In ‘work’ and ‘skin’, the earlier **r** must first be removed from between the vowel and nasal, or the conditioning must include that cluster. In ‘five’, some varieties retain nasalization after losing the nasal consonant. [Schmidt and Kaul 2008: 258–264, 271, 275](https://www.wisdomlib.org/uploads/journals/acta-orie/vol-69-2008/7372-6759-23258.pdf).

This is a useful correspondence, still short of an exceptionless rule. Gilgiti **khaṇ** ‘mountain’ and Kohistani **khoṇ ~ khaṇ** preserve variation in an apparently relevant environment. Gilgiti **panzéy** ‘fifteen’ retains pretonic **a**, but Guresi ‘thumb’ begins with pretonic **o**, so an unqualified restriction to accented vowels will not cover both. Different compound histories, intervening vowels, and nasal loss need to be controlled. Long **ā** has additional dialectal developments: **mā́sa-** gives Gilgiti **máaz** beside Kohistani **móos**. Neither a general Shina **a → o** nor a family-wide copy of Palula's raising chain is justified. The companion table keeps the nasal correspondence and these limits together.

**Aspiration can move before it disappears.** Palula **ghoóṣṭ** ‘house’ places aspiration earlier than **gōṣṭhá-**. The grammar records variants with aspiration in different positions within the same word. Sawi **beeṭhu** ‘sat’ retains internal aspiration beside Palula **bhéṭu**; this comparison cannot be described simply as Sawi deleting Palula's initial aspiration. Some Palula aspiration also lacks an aspirated Old Indo-Aryan counterpart. Its date must be established before it is used to predict Kalkoti low tone. [Liljegren 2016: 69–72](https://doi.org/10.17169/langsci.b82.85), [Liljegren 2009: 34–35](https://doi.org/10.5617/ao.5341).

**Glides and consonant loss create new long vowels.** Palula **nóo** ‘name’, with inflected **nóowa**, belongs to a history involving **m → w** and loss of final **w**, rather than just **ā → oo**. The diphthong reconstructed for ‘father-in-law’ becomes Ashret **úu** but Biori **áa**; the related collective **ša(w)úra** ‘father-in-law’s household’ preserves evidence for the glide. Internal vowel contact can also merge into a long vowel after **h** loss, as in the Shina ‘light’ and ‘red’ comparisons. The accent of a contraction depends on the input vowels and their prominence. A single rule that labels every contracted vowel rising would fail on early-accented **nóo**. [Liljegren 2009: 21–25, 36–38](https://doi.org/10.5617/ao.5341).

**Sawi's quantity system has been reorganized.** In Liljegren's 2009 western comparison, old long **aa** corresponds to **o**, older **oo** to **u**, and older **ee** to **i**; the high-vowel length distinctions subsequently merge. Knobloch analyzes only **a/aː** as a pure vowel-length opposition. Her orthographic **ee** represents /e/, contrasting in quality with **e** /ɛ/: doubling is not a uniform instruction to reconstruct two morae. Such mergers can remove environments in which an inherited mora contrast was expressed. They do not prove that vowel merger caused the loss or restructuring of accent, especially while long **aa** remains. [Liljegren 2009: 38–42](https://doi.org/10.5617/ao.5341), [Knobloch 2020: 14–15](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2%3A1440556/FULLTEXT01.pdf).

The Sawi chain also needs lexical checking against later descriptions. Liljegren's table has **nom** ‘name’, whereas Knobloch's consonant table gives **naːm**. Dialect variation, loan replacement, and differences in recording remain possible; these are not successive recordings of the same speaker demonstrating **o → aa**. The discrepancy is a limit on treating the earlier comparative table as an exceptionless modern dictionary.

**Kalkoti preserves older vowel qualities through much of this western raising.** Its **taar** ‘star’, **goor** ‘horse’, and **eer** ‘sheep’ retain the older **aa**, **oo**, and **ee** respectively, where Palula has undergone raising. Short **a** nevertheless lengthened in both open and closed environments, as in **taat** ‘hot’, **baaṭ** ‘stone’, and **kaan** ‘ear’. These are Liljegren's 2009 segmental comparisons, written without a reliable tonal analysis at that stage; the later tone evidence is considered separately below. Thus similar long **aa** in Palula and Kalkoti does not entail the same chronology or contour. Kalkoti's front **ää** also includes proposed monophthongizations of **ai**, alongside umlaut and contact vocabulary. An exhaustive derivation of that vowel requires distinguishing those sources. [Liljegren 2009: 39–40](https://doi.org/10.5617/ao.5341), [Liljegren 2013: 130–133](https://journals.dartmouth.edu/journals/xmlpage/1/article/423?htmlOnce=yes).

These changes are part of the explanation wherever they create or remove the syllable, vowel, or aspiration that conditions an accent rule. Additional consonant correspondences can establish cognacy without themselves explaining accent; there is no gain in calling each of them tonogenesis.

## Inflection preserves distinctions that citation forms conceal

Kohistani Shina's noun paradigms make a restricted accent-shift rule quite clear: **caár → caré** ‘direction’ and **kaál → kalí** ‘year’ move accent from the second mora of a long vowel to the ending, shortening the newly unaccented root. Early-accented **góoṣ → góoẓi** ‘house’ and **núum → núumi** ‘name’ retain it. Short vowels do not offer an equivalent prediction: **don → dódi** ‘tooth’ is fixed, but **don → doní** ‘flock’ is mobile. Moreover, Kohistani **ǰip → ǰíba** ‘tongue’ differs from mobile Palula **ǰip → ǰipí**, despite their shared comparison with **jihvā́-**. [Schmidt and Kohistani 2008: 47–50](https://books.google.com/books?id=mZzD86umtbAC&pg=PA49).

This requires a separate quantity mechanism: **long vowels can shorten when accent moves away**. Palula also shortens **áai → ai** when its infinitive becomes a verbal noun with accented **-nií**, as in **utrapáai → utrapainií** ‘run’. The restriction matters. Palula **deés/deesá** keeps the long root vowel, whereas **báaṭ/baṭá** alternates; some apparent shortening can also be analyzed historically as lengthening confined to the accented form. We need class- and formation-specific histories, not an instruction to shorten every unaccented long vowel throughout Shinaic. [Liljegren 2016: 79–80, 233](https://doi.org/10.17169/langsci.b82.85).

This is why a reconstruction needs lexical accent classes as well as sound changes. An old ending can disappear and leave its accent behind; a later paradigm can then generalize either a fixed or a mobile pattern. “Analogy” becomes an explanation only when we identify the forms that supply the model. Without that evidence it remains the name of a possible mechanism, and should not be used to erase a counterexample.

Verbs bring their own accent-bearing suffixes. In both Shina and Palula, a later accented suffix can supersede the stem's accent, sometimes shortening the root vowel. Comparing a Shina infinitive with a Sanskrit present-tense citation therefore tests different formations. Kohistani **cáram** ‘I might graze’ and **carám** ‘I might take [animals] for grazing’ show why the distinction matters: the second accent marks a transitive formation. The corresponding past forms are **carílos** and **caráas**. Neither accent placement nor the past-tense endings can be reconstructed by treating the two verbs as interchangeable reflexes of the same Sanskrit root. [Schmidt and Kohistani 2008: 135](https://books.google.com/books?id=mZzD86umtbAC&pg=PA135).

The same grammar provides an unusually concrete example of remodeling: it considers **khyeé** ‘having eaten’ and **khyáao** ‘he ate’ analogical to **pyeé**, **pyáao** ‘drink’. The inherited root of ‘eat’, **khā́d-**, does not independently explain the inserted glide. A small morphological analogy does more explanatory work here than a new sound change invented for one verb. [Schmidt and Kohistani 2008: 176](https://books.google.com/books?id=mZzD86umtbAC&pg=PA176).

Even the apocope examples vary. In the same comparative list, ‘day’ is Gilgiti **déez**, Kohistani **deés**, Guresi **dées**, while Palula has **deés**. Degener independently records Gilgiti [**dées**](form:f_jokae57ehw4sg), supporting the early accent despite the difference in the final consonant. A rule that retracts every lost final accent to the last surviving mora explains some of these forms and fails on others. Earlier retraction and the history of the inflectional class remain live possibilities. A shared origin of the accent system does not imply identical later development of every noun.

‘House’ exposes a different problem: the article disagrees with itself. Its Table 1 has Gilgiti **goóṭ**, but Table 2 has **góoṭ**; the two tables also disagree over Astori. Both [Degener](form:f_oetdadrtngayc) and [Buddruss](form:f_yyt3dcsldeqt6) independently support Gilgiti **goóṭ**. I therefore use the late Gilgiti accent as the better-supported reading and exclude the conflicting entry from tests of a sound law. Kohistani **góoṣ**, however, has early accent in both tables, so the broader historical problem survives this correction. Source criticism removes a false complication without removing the real one. [Schmidt and Kaul 2008: 237, 263, 265](https://www.wisdomlib.org/uploads/journals/acta-orie/vol-69-2008/7372-6759-23258.pdf).

The paradigms supply a further test. In Tileli, **ẓuúk → ẓukí** ‘kidney’ and **ḍeér → ḍereé** ‘belly’ look like regular movement from the last stem mora to an ending. But **don → dóni** ‘tooth’ and **mũyál → mũyále** ‘earthquake’ retain stem accent, while **kúṭu → kuṭé** ‘knee’ shifts despite its earlier accent. The authors explicitly reject a consistent automatic rule on their available sample. Their Guresi and Tileli paradigms also confirm fixed accent in ‘house’. This evidence favors reconstructing accentual classes and subsequent analogical changes, rather than assuming that a citation form mechanically predicts every inflection. [Schmidt and Kaul 2010: 197–200](https://journal.fi/store/article/view/52389).

## Kundal Shahi: deletion, umlaut, and an accent beyond the word

Kundal Shahi makes the importance of whole paradigms particularly clear. **Báal** ‘hair’ retains its accent in **báalan**, whereas **baán** ‘dish’ has **baanán**. **Díiz** ‘day’ is fixed, adding a further relative with early accent to the comparison above. The ordinary long-vowel contrast can therefore be analyzed in terms similar to Shina's, but that is only the beginning of the evidence. [Rehman and Baart 2005: 12–14](https://www.sil.org/system/files/reapdata/10/55/83/10558360034839761273787716304857102032/silewp2005_008.pdf).

A set of feminine nouns retains accented **-íi** in the nominative plural and oblique singular, but shortens it to **-í** or loses it in the nominative singular. The surviving stem may also show umlaut, revealing the influence of an ending which is no longer pronounced:

| Fuller form | Shortened nominative singular | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **thuuníi** ‘pillar’ | **thuún** | A rise remains on the preceding long vowel |
| **almaaríi** ‘cupboard’ | **almɛɛ́r** | The long vowel is fronted and receives late accent |
| **batíi** ‘lamp’ | **bɛ̀t**, also **bɛtí** | Fronting survives; complete deletion leaves a low short vowel |
| **kursíi** ‘chair’ | **kürsí** | The final vowel shortens, while the preceding vowel fronts |

The cupboard, lamp, and chair forms are especially useful because the same mechanism affects recognizable later vocabulary. We do not have to reconstruct every detail from Vedic. I have omitted the source's redundant low mark before a rising long vowel in this table; the grave on **bɛ̀t** is essential. The authors describe these low forms as potentially *post-accented*: the accent belongs after the last pronounced stem vowel. A long vowel can host a late peak; a short vowel need not simply turn high when the following accented vowel disappears.

The two effects of the lost ending should be distinguished: **umlaut preserves its vowel quality's influence, while the pitch pattern preserves its accentual influence**. Their coexistence supports deletion after those influences became established. It does not date this productive truncation to the same period as the much older loss of endings in ‘son’ or ‘house’.

There are also post-accenting nouns without this visible **-íi** alternation. The paper lists ‘button’, ‘face’, ‘plough’, ‘ox’, and ‘shoe’, whose accent appears on the oblique plural suffix or a following postposition, and explicitly leaves their explanation open. A theory that gives every Kundal Shahi word exactly one pronounced high mora would miss these data. Conversely, turning every such form into a separate primitive tone would discard the connection between tone and inflection. The best current account needs a lexical accent that can be realized beyond the stem, with the history of individual classes still to be established.

## Kalkoti: a new tonal system over an older one

Kalkoti requires a different analysis from Palula. In Hultman's description, a long vowel can be unmarked for tone, have high tone on its first mora, have high tone on its second mora, have low tone, or combine low and high. Low tone behaves as a property of the syllable; high tone has a more specific mora position. A word can have no high tone or more than one, as in **míšaalúm** ‘men, oblique’. These are consequences of a system built from high and low tones and their associations, rather than five unrelated accents. [Hultman 2023: 14–20](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2%3A1772061/FULLTEXT01.pdf).

One innovation is particularly clear. Palula **bháanu** ‘becomes’ corresponds to Kalkoti **bùun**, while Kalkoti **buun** ‘goes’ lacks the low tone. Loss of voiced aspiration has left a lexical pitch distinction. Further comparisons include **ḍä̀är** ‘belly’ beside Palula **ḍheér**, **gìin** ‘took’ beside **ghíinu**, and **lùun** ‘salt’ beside **lhoóṇ**. Voiceless aspiration, which survives in Kalkoti, also lowers pitch; where the consonant predicts the low pitch, an additional lexical tone specification is unnecessary.

A second innovation accompanies the loss of final unaccented vowels. Kalkoti **taár** ‘star’, **deéd** ‘paternal grandmother’, and **yeé** ‘mother’ correspond to Palula **tóoru**, **déedi**, and **yéei**. These are **not** examples of an old final accent simply surviving the deletion of its vowel: the Palula comparanda have early accent and an unaccented ending. Liljegren instead proposes that apocope introduced glottalization and a new final high tone. In present-day Kalkoti, a final high tone is often realized as creak or glottalization at the end of an utterance, but as rising pitch before following material. That alternation makes the proposed connection plausible, while leaving the historical direction between glottalization and tone less secure. [Liljegren 2013: 143–144](https://journals.dartmouth.edu/journals/xmlpage/1/article/423?htmlOnce=yes).

The older accent layer is harder to reconstruct. Hultman's examples show this pattern:

| Palula comparison | Kalkoti | Possible historical relation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **dáand**, **ráat**, **bíi** ‘tooth, blood, seed’ | **daan**, **raat**, **bii** | Old early accent becomes unmarked |
| **šaák**, **poó**, **rhoó** ‘wood, boy, song’ | **šáak**, **púu**, **róo** | Old late accent corresponds to new early high tone |
| **tóoru**, **déedi** ‘star, paternal grandmother’ | **taár**, **deéd** | New final high tone associated with apocope |

This is an attractive ordering: simplify or retime the inherited contrast, then add new final high tones when endings disappear. It is stronger than an account that derives every Kalkoti tone from lost aspiration, since aspiration alone cannot explain the distinction between early and late high tone. It is also more cautious than saying that Kalkoti first lost every trace of inherited accent. The correspondence involving old late accent is small, and the analyses disagree over individual words: Liljegren's ‘wood’ has a final low tone, whereas Hultman's has early high tone. The observations do not establish a sound change occurring between the publication dates.

There are further limits. **Meéš** ‘man’ has rising tone without the expected final vowel in Palula **míiš**. **Ràat** ‘night’ has low tone without an aspirated onset in Palula **raát**. Conversely, Palula's aspirated sonorants in **mhaás** ‘meat’, **nhiáaṛa** ‘near’, and **rhoó** ‘song’ do not correspond to low tone in the Kalkoti forms. Gawri contact is a plausible explanation for some of these differences, but resemblance to Gawri is not sufficient to identify a particular word as borrowed. Another possibility to test is that some aspiration was introduced in Palula after the varieties separated.

Hultman also makes a methodological qualification that must survive into any historical account: an unmarked form in the thesis can reflect uncertainty about the recording, rather than a confident judgment that the word is toneless. The examples above are his explicit comparisons; a count of every unmarked word in the extracted glossary would not measure historical loss of accent.

## Where the available evidence stops

For **Sawi/Sauji**, Gomes's acoustic study—71 nouns from one speaker—found substantial evidence against retention of a Palula-like system. Only 28 basic forms and 26 inflected forms matched the Palula contours; most matches coincided with the common Sawi pattern of prominence near the word's end. The author concludes that the recorded nouns lack Palula-like lexical pitch accent, while explicitly leaving room for a different tonal system. This supports loss or substantial restructuring more strongly than mere absence of tone marks would. Its limits are one speaker, nouns only, and a restricted framing procedure. The reported significance tests establish a majority of mismatches, not a historical model of which accent changes produced them. Knobloch's later sketch leaves stress and pitch accent unanalyzed. Complete accent loss remains a hypothesis to test beyond this corpus. [Gomes 2014: 19–23](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2%3A1055060/FULLTEXT01.pdf), [Knobloch 2020: 15](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2%3A1440556/FULLTEXT01.pdf).

More recent connected-text evidence deserves attention without being made to say too much. Liljegren's 2025 comparative abstract writes Sauji **pi-eé** ‘boy, oblique’ and **ǰandraw-eé** ‘snake, oblique’, alongside accent-marked Brokskat forms. It does not analyze the prosodic contrasts or supply a minimal pair. These transcriptions identify material for a new test; their acute marks alone neither refute Gomes's acoustic findings nor prove that Sauji has the full Palula system. [Liljegren 2025, abstract, examples 1–2](https://researchmap.jp/sakuraishikawa/presentations/51401293/attachment_file.pdf).

For **Ushojo**, Baart's published survey places it among the Shina-type systems and reports accent advancement; his preliminary comparison emphasizes that this persists despite contact with Torwali. That is a useful contrast with Kalkoti, whose system more closely resembles neighboring Gawri. Contact is a historical circumstance to investigate, not a law that mechanically assigns a language its neighbor's tones. The accessible descriptions do not supply enough accent-marked cognates and paradigms to establish a Ushojo-specific chronology. [Baart 2014, *Tone and stress in North-West Indo-Aryan: A survey*](https://doi.org/10.1075/z.189.01baa), [Baart, “More on Tone in North-West Indo-Aryan: Ushojo and Khalkoti”](https://sil.academia.edu/JoanBaart/Talks).

Decker's earlier survey makes the gap explicit. It did not attempt a tonal analysis, although instrumental inspection suggested a tonal factor; a distinctiveness claim remained tentative. It also found voiced aspiration barely perceptible, if present at all, with a few uncertain tokens from speakers of different ages. The older vocabulary therefore cannot establish a toneless Ushojo stage, nor can its uncertain aspirates date a sound change to one generation. Its **doːn** ‘teeth’, **noːm** ‘name’, and **ˈɽɑːti** ‘night’ remain useful segmental evidence, with the apostrophe recording syllable stress. [Decker 1992: 65, 72–75, 193–194](https://www.sil.org/system/files/reapdata/10/84/73/108473746620733697887240640040710459748/32836_SSNP01.pdf).

For **Brokskat**, Schmidt and Kaul heard falling contours on some long vowels but did not detect rising ones, and could not exclude list intonation as the source of the falls. Accordingly, they marked stress rather than pitch accent in their Brokskat list. A loss or merger of the inherited contour contrast is a reasonable hypothesis; the list alone does not prove the absence of all lexical prosodic distinctions throughout Brokskat. This distinction between evidence of absence and absence of measurement prevents a tidy family tree from outrunning its sources. [Schmidt and Kaul 2008: 234](https://www.wisdomlib.org/uploads/journals/acta-orie/vol-69-2008/7372-6759-23258.pdf).

Sharma's independent Brokskat grammar makes the uncertainty more concrete. It treats most vowel length as predictable from phonetic environment, admitting contrastive length principally for **i** and **u**. Such an analysis would leave less room for the familiar Shina contrast between the two morae of a long vowel. But its examples are not an acoustic demonstration that the old accent opposition merged, and its sound-system chapter does not provide the missing accentual paradigms. It is evidence for a competing description, not a dated sound law. [Sharma 1998: 33–35](https://pahar.in/indian-subcontinent-books/).

The printed tables also correct a misleading digital reading. Brokskat ‘hot’ is **ˈtaato** on Schmidt and Kaul's p. 276, with syllable stress, whereas Jambu's current source transcription has **taáto**, which looks like second-mora accent. The original does not supply evidence for a Brokskat rising tone here. I use the printed reading in this comparison and record the transcription discrepancy separately.

**Drasi** illustrates the reverse danger. Rajapurohit's grammar calls its acute mark *stress*, contrasting **áʐo** ‘cloud’ with **aʐó** ‘inside’, and treats identical-vowel sequences with different accent positions among its diphthongs. This vocabulary grew from fieldwork in the 1970s, although the book appeared in 2012. Its terminology does not by itself contradict the mora contrast heard in Drasi by Schmidt and Kaul. We should not infer tone loss just because one description uses the word “stress.” [Rajapurohit 2012: 28–33](https://www.aa.tufs.ac.jp/~tjun/shina/Grammar_of_Shina_Language_And_Vocabulary.pdf).

## Two cognate families across the group

‘Tooth’ and ‘name’ illustrate both the reach and the limits of a family-wide comparison. Their Old Indo-Aryan comparisons, **dánta-** and **nā́man-**, have stem accent, but their modern vowel qualities, lengths, final consonants and inflectional forms differ. The table retains each source's notation; **ˈ** marks syllable stress, and the absence of an accent mark means only that the cited form does not specify it. Kalkoti **daan** is an exception to that last caution: it is explicitly used as an unmarked-tone comparison in Hultman's analysis.

## Etymology is part of the accent argument

An accent mismatch can expose a wrong comparison rather than a new sound change. This is particularly important when a dictionary groups several Sanskrit formations under one entry number. The first printed headword is not automatically the immediate ancestor of every modern form.

**‘Eye’: start from a dual.** Palula [**ac̣híi**](form:f_hkqbpcmffxfsu) and the corresponding Shina forms disagree with the initial accent of Sanskrit [**ákṣi**](entry:43). But Old Indo-Aryan also has the attested dual **akṣī́**, with exactly the final long accented vowel that the modern comparison needs. Lexicalization of a paired body-part form offers a more economical starting point than an otherwise unmotivated accent shift in **ákṣi**. The cluster development still has to be accounted for, and the modern feminine singular requires a change in number and gender. Those are real costs of the proposal. Nevertheless, the alternative starts from an attested form and predicts both accent and length, so I prefer it as a working etymology. I do not claim that the dual explanation is new to scholarship. [Monier-Williams, s.v. *akṣi*, with the accented dual](https://michaelmeyer.fr/sanskrit/meta/terms/ak%E1%B9%A3i).

**‘Load’: choose between two old vowels.** Palula [**bháaru**](form:f_lybl5xubglnam) is usually compared with the long-vowel **bhārá-** family. Yet Sanskrit also has [**bhára-**](entry:9393), with a short, initially accented vowel. An extended formation based on short **bhára-** would undergo open-syllable lengthening to **bháaru**; old long **ā**, if subjected to the same early chain as other inherited stems, would instead be expected to raise. Accent and vowel quality thus favor considering the short-vowel base together. The difficulty is morphological: an appropriate extended **bhára-** formation must be justified, while **bhāraka-** with long **ā** is attested. This is a tentative preference between etymological bases, not a reconstruction secure enough to change the dictionary link. [Turner's two families, **bhára-**](entry:9393) and [**bhārá-**](entry:9459).

**‘Self’: consider a loan before an accent exception.** The dictionary connects [**zaán**](form:f_y72fzcb66idm2) with Sanskrit **jána-** ‘person’. But Palula separately has **ǰáanu** ‘person’, the more transparent inherited comparison. For **zaán**, the long vowel, late accent and **z** make an Iranian loan worth testing. Persian/Urdu **jān** includes ‘self’ among its meanings, and Pashto **ځان** is the ordinary reflexive ‘self’. Either offers a long vowel without requiring a new native lengthening rule. [Platts 1884, s.v. **jān**](https://www.rekhta.org/urdudictionary?keyword=jaa.n), [Pashto reflexive **ځان**](https://universaldependencies.org/ps/feat/PronType.html).

The counterevidence prevents a confident choice of donor. The Palula vocabulary gives **ǰaán** as a variant and **ǰaní** as its oblique: the word participates in native mobile inflection. Loan adaptation could produce that paradigm, but it has not been demonstrated here. There is a concrete consonantal control: Palula **zhaáy** ‘place; property’ is compared with Pashto **dzāˈe**, and inflects as **zhayí**. Pashto's affricate can therefore correspond to **zh**, rather than simply **z** or **ǰ**. This does not exclude a different dialect or borrowing period for ‘self’, but makes that additional history necessary. Pashto matches the reflexive meaning particularly well; Persian/Urdu matches the **ǰ** variant more directly. My proposal is therefore **a loan hypothesis for zaán**, with the donor unresolved. It explains why the word should not be counted as secure evidence that inherited **jána-** changed its accent. [Liljegren and Haider 2011, s.vv. **zaán**, **zhaáy**](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2%3A461738/FULLTEXT01.pdf).

Some apparent discoveries disappear on inspection. Palula [**róot**](form:f_7375t3pe4rnl2) ‘night’ already belongs to Turner's **rā́trī-** family; recognizing that connection is not a new etymology. What remains unexplained is its relation to the other Palula noun **raát**, with different accent, vowel quality and inflection. Likewise, **díir** ‘husband's brother’ can be compared with the accented variant **dḗvara-**, and **tóoru** ‘star’ with **tā́rakā-** or **tā́ra-**, rather than an unrelated accented formation chosen merely because it appears first in an entry.

Even the missing **r** in ‘night’ has a specific proposed explanation. Morgenstierne reconstructs dissimilated **\*rātti**, allowing **tr → tt** in a word that already begins with **r**, while Palula otherwise retains many postconsonantal **r** sounds. This supplies a possible consonantal path to **róot** without predicting loss of **r** in **putr** ‘son’ or **c̣híitr** ‘field’. It does not explain the late accent of **raát**, and the reconstructed intermediate must remain distinct from an attested form. [Morgenstierne 1941: 15, §20](https://mahraka.com/pdf/Notes%20on%20Phalura.pdf).

**‘Boy’ cautions against making a reconstruction obligatory.** Liljegren derives **phoó** through **\*pháa → \*phóo → phoó**, which places accent advancement after raising. That is a valid ordering *if the reconstructed aa stage is correct*. The dictionary instead links the word to Turner's **\*phōta-**, within the **pṓta-** family, where long **o** is already part of the proposed input. Its unaccented reconstructed headword does not establish an old late accent, and the irregular plural **phaayá** still needs an explanation. But it means that **phoó** alone cannot prove the aa-to-oo stage or its proposed subsequent accent shift. The useful conclusion is conditional, not a new universal law for final **oo**. [Liljegren 2009: 24](https://doi.org/10.5617/ao.5341), [Turner 8399](entry:8399), [Liljegren 2016: 124](https://doi.org/10.17169/langsci.b82.85).

## Could some differences be older than Vedic?

There is a serious alternative to assuming that every disagreement arose inside Shinaic. Dybo argues that some fixed-accent Shina nouns preserve an older accent which Vedic shifted. He compares, among others, ‘mill’, ‘kidney’, ‘sleep’, ‘cloud’ and ‘poison’, drawing support from other Indo-European branches. This is a historical hypothesis about the input to Shinaic, not just another modern accent rule. [Dybo 2011: 202–206](https://www.rsuh.ru/upload/main/vestnik/fvir/Vestnik-16_11.pdf).

The hypothesis deserves consideration because Vedic is a sister witness to the relevant earlier Indo-Aryan dialects, not a recording of every Shinaic ancestor. A reconstruction should not assume a Vedic innovation was necessarily shared. However, agreement among several modern Shinaic languages can also reflect a common Shinaic innovation, and an added derivational ending can replace accent without preserving a pre-Vedic pattern. The external comparisons and the morphology must do the discriminating work.

The expanded family comparison supplies direct opposition. Palula [**biṣ → biṣá**](form:f_owbhgyb6dni5e) ‘poison’ is mobile, whereas Dybo's Gilgiti comparison is fixed; the old **viṣá-** accent therefore still fits one Shinaic paradigm. ‘Tongue’ has the same problem: mobile Palula beside fixed Kohistani and Gilgiti. Even ‘kidney’ varies in vowel length and accent across Shina varieties. These words cannot all be assigned an unchanged, family-wide fixed paradigm by citing Vedic's supposed innovation.

Kulikov's reply challenges parts of Dybo's **verbal** reconstruction, especially etymological and morphological identifications. That criticism does not automatically refute every nominal comparison. Conversely, a plausible nominal case does not validate the whole reconstructed verbal system. The economical position is to allow pre-Vedic accent differences where independently supported, while leaving the disputed words outside the secure sound-law evidence. [Kulikov 2011: 210–211, in the same discussion](https://www.rsuh.ru/upload/main/vestnik/fvir/Vestnik-16_11.pdf).

<h2 id="the-smallest-account-the-evidence-supports">The smallest account the evidence supports</h2>

We need an inherited lexical accent as the starting condition. We also need to compare equivalent formations: an accented derivational or inflectional ending can supersede an old accent. Neither inheritance nor morphological accent assignment is a sound change. Keeping them outside the list prevents us from inventing a phonetic rule whenever two different formations disagree.

With that distinction, the necessary changes can be reduced to the following nine groups of mechanisms. Each row can contain several branch-specific sound changes: the [detailed rule ledger](../research/shinaic-accent/rule-ledger.tsv) separates their inputs, conditions, relative order and evidential status. The table is ordered within each branch where the evidence permits it; it is **not** a single sequence through which every Shinaic language passed.

| Change | Domain and ordering | Evidence and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Delete final vowels; retain or reassociate their accent.** In the Palula mobile nouns, a lost final accent docks on the last surviving vowel mora. | An early loss of endings precedes the later Palula treatment of closed syllables. Corresponding losses occur in Shina, with subsequent class differences. | **deés/deesá**, **putr/putrá**, Shina **moós**. Does not predict fixed ‘day’, ‘house’, or ‘tongue’ in every dialect. |
| **Shorten a long vowel when its accent is displaced.** | Particular Shina inflectional classes and Palula formations; the accent shift must precede or accompany shortening. | Kohistani **kaál/kalí**, Palula **utrapáai/utrapainií**. Retained **deesá** excludes universal shortening; some other alternations instead continue accent-conditioned lengthening. |
| **Reduce clusters and consonants; contract adjacent vowels where appropriate.** Preserve the distinction between a surviving accented vowel and a later accented vowel drawn into a contraction. | Branch-specific **r** metathesis, retroflexion, geminate reduction, nasal-cluster changes, aspiration movement and **h/w** loss create the inputs. | ‘Hot’, ‘light’, ‘red’, and ‘name’. Compensation is established for particular histories, not every deleted consonant. |
| **Lengthen accented short a and shift older early-accented long vowels.** Core Palula open-syllable **á → áa**, **áa → óo**, **ée → íi**, **óo → úu**; Biori also takes the new **oo** to **uu**. | Working Palula model: an open-syllable chain, followed by an Ashret extension into closed syllables. Old and newly created vowels remain distinct. Sawi has a related chain plus high-vowel quantity mergers; Kalkoti lengthens **a** but retains older **aa/ee/oo** in the comparisons above. | Biori **kram/kráama**, Ashret **kráam/kráama**, and the long-vowel correspondences. Internal nasal clusters require an earlier structural account or analogical extension; parallel branch changes remain possible. |
| **Give newly lengthened closed-syllable a a late accent after aspiration or a preceding unaccented syllable; otherwise give it early accent.** | Later Ashret layer; Biori lacks this general closed-syllable lengthening. Independently, Biori raises early closed **óo → úu**. | **haát/háata**, **basaánd**, **báaṭ/baṭá**. The conditioning describes nominal evidence especially well; **kháaṇ**, ‘grape’, and the imperative paradigm limit its historical generality. |
| **Front a back vowel before a following i; allow the trigger to be lost later.** | An older Palula layer follows **ée → íi** and probably precedes **áa → óo**; later forms extend the alternation. Kundal Shahi and Kalkoti also preserve fronting before deleted endings. | **dóodo/déedi**, **táatu/téeti**, **kitaáb/kiteebí**, Kundal Shahi **almaaríi/almɛɛ́r**. Similar umlaut does not alone prove one shared innovation. |
| **Replace lost voiced aspiration or h with low tone.** | Kalkoti; retained voiceless aspiration also causes predictable low pitch. | **bùun** ‘becomes’ versus **buun** ‘goes’, plus **gìin**, **lùun**. Palula **mh/nh/rh** comparisons are not uniformly low and require separate histories. |
| **Introduce final high tone with a later loss of unaccented final vowels.** | Kalkoti, after the old contrasts had acquired their Kalkoti correspondences. Connection with glottalization is supported, but its causal direction is less secure. | **tóoru : taár**, **déedi : deéd**, **yéei : yeé**. This is a new accent, distinct from carrying forward an old final accent. |
| **Shorten or delete accented final íi, preserving its effects on the preceding stem.** | Productive Kundal Shahi truncation; umlaut precedes complete loss. | **thuuníi/thuún**, **batíi/bɛ̀t**, **kursíi/kürsí**. Short stems can remain low or post-accenting, so Palula's docking rule cannot simply be exported. |

Several extensions remain provisional. The Kalkoti correspondence **old early accent → unmarked; old late accent → early H** is promising but still rests on a small explicitly compared sample. Accent attraction to an independently old long vowel, as in ‘fire’, is a plausible earlier rule; more controlled families are needed to define its scope. Shina **a → o** in nasal environments also remains a restricted correspondence with unresolved conditioning. Loans may have reinforced Palula's late-accent pattern; they have not been shown to create every inherited-looking contrast. Sawi and Brokskat probably lost or substantially reorganized parts of the inherited contrast, but the available evidence does not identify a complete merger rule. Ushojo retains a reported Shina-type system; its individual historical changes remain underdocumented.

This account makes concrete ordering claims without pretending to recover a fully dated prehistory. The environment for lengthening must exist before lengthening; aspiration must still be present when it conditions a vowel or tone, even if it disappears later; an umlaut trigger must exist before the fronting it causes; and an old accent must have a different history from a high tone introduced by later apocope. It does **not** require every raising to occur in a discrete stage, every similarity to be inherited from the same intermediate ancestor, or every irregular noun to instantiate a new sound law.

<h2 id="the-remaining-counterexamples-are-part-of-the-result">The remaining counterexamples are part of the result</h2>

The following are unresolved problems, not entries silently excluded to make the rules work.

| Problem | Why the current account is insufficient | What would distinguish the alternatives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Palula **díiš** ‘village’ | Fixed accent and raised **ii** against **dēśá-** require a change before raising. | Older paradigms, a justified alternative formation, or evidence for a loan. |
| Biori **dan/dáanda**, **náang/núunga** | Lengthening or raising occurs before an internal nasal cluster, complicating a literal modern open-syllable rule. | Earlier syllabification versus analogical spread of the alternation from transparent **kaṇ/káaṇa**, **kram/kráama**. |
| Palula **kháaṇ** ‘mountain’, **dhráac̣** ‘grape’ | The modern aspiration and length do not follow the simplest late-lengthening prediction. | Earlier quantity and aspiration position, including Biori short forms; changing the Sanskrit label alone is insufficient. |
| Palula **raát** and **róot** ‘night’ | One has late **aa**, the other early raised **oo**, despite their connection to the same old family. | Separate formation or borrowing histories, checked against their different inflections. |
| Fixed Palula **yáandr**, **níindra**, **ghróoṇ**, **nóong** | The cited **yantrá-**, **nidrā́-**, **ghrāṇá-**, **nakhá-** do not mechanically predict the modern accent. | Independent evidence for pre-Vedic accent, derivation, or analogical models; the last two also need careful segmental reconstruction. |
| Palula **dhrúuk**, **phiíṇ**, **wíi**, **mung** | ‘Stream’ has unexplained early **uu**; ‘steam’ has late **ii** and mobile inflection against **phḗna-**; ‘water’ has fixed early accent after extensive contraction; ‘bean’ is fixed against **mudgá-**. | Quantity and contraction histories, alternative formations, and independently identifiable models for class changes. These belong to the wider nominal audit, not the restricted **aa** test. |
| Mobile Palula **sáar**, **ṣáak** | Their inflection disagrees with a simple fixed-class prediction from **sáras-**, **srákva-**. | The sources of the modern case endings and the history of class assignment. |
| Shina ‘day’, ‘house’, ‘tongue’, ‘kidney’ | Dialects preserve different contours or different fixed/mobile classes after obvious transcription conflicts are removed. | Matched paradigms from named localities, rather than pooled citation forms. |
| Strand's Palula **pʹûtr/pʹûtra**, **ghâʹnu**, and aggregate accent counts | Independent observations disagree with the default description; the published count does not separate all relevant vowel and formation histories. | Speaker-matched recordings, individual etymologies for the counted words, and loan stratification. |
| Kalkoti **meéš**, **ràat** and the aspirated-sonorant comparisons | New high or low tones lack the expected Palula conditioning. | Matched Gawri and Kalkoti paradigms, loan strata, and the chronology of secondary Palula aspiration. |
| Palula compounds and extended kinship terms | The teens' regular **áa/óo**, **ée/íi** alternations require earlier contracted stems; **ǰhaamatroó** ‘son-in-law’ and **phoó** ‘boy’ have additional suffix histories. | Test analogical teen-ending accent against the ‘twelve/eighteen’ models; reconstruct the individual contractions. In **phoó**, establish an earlier **aa** stage against the **\*phōta-** alternative. |

A useful next discovery would eliminate several of these rows with one independently supported condition. Until then, keeping them visible is more economical than multiplying exceptions disguised as sound changes.

## Evidence, selection, and attribution

The [downloadable evidence package](../research/shinaic-accent/shinaic-accent-evidence.zip) contains the selected forms, manual assessments, source-page checks, rule ledger and reproducible analysis scripts. The [appendix guide](../research/shinaic-accent/README.md) explains the selection and notation; the [manifest](../research/shinaic-accent/manifest.json) records exact input hashes. The underlying sources were already present in Jambu. This investigation adds a historical assessment, without replacing their accepted etymologies or silently correcting their transcriptions.

The Palula inventory has **2,700 main entries**, of which **511 have Turner links**. A restricted screen selects **187 nominal, adjectival and numeral entries** with an explicitly accented first proto-form token; all receive a manual assessment. It excludes unlinked vocabulary, unaccented source labels and many alternative formations contained later in an etymological entry. The parallel Shina screen covers **139 accented Turner families** in a source with six named varieties, while the broader regional comparison selects **692 survey rows**, representing **23 concepts in 27 localities**. These are measures of coverage, not independent confirmations or percentages of explained history. The separate **36-form short-a test** and **34 imperative forms** answer narrower questions, as described above.

Central correspondences were checked against the original pages, particularly where a diacritic changes the argument. The [source-reading table](../research/shinaic-accent/source-readings.tsv) records six digital discrepancies or qualifications, including Brokskat stress and the long final **ī** in Turner's **nāḍī́**. The larger manually annotated tables remain a first-pass historical screen; they do not claim page-image verification of every entry. A source's blank accent field, a conjectured Sanskrit form, and a modern measured contour have different evidential status throughout.

The Palula forms and original etymological suggestions are chiefly Henrik Liljegren's, with the contribution of Naseem Haider to the vocabulary and documentation. Richard Strand supplies independent Palula observations, an explicit vowel chronology, and the competing loan account. The comparative Shina lists are Ruth Laila Schmidt and Vijay Kumar Kaul's; the Kohistani grammar is Schmidt and Razwal Kohistani's; the regional survey is Carla F. Radloff's contribution to *Languages of Northern Areas*. The language-specific descriptions are credited alongside their claims. The PAHAR links lead to its catalogue: search the cited author or book title and use the download link there, since direct PDF links are blocked. Basu's 2025 abstract supplies the explicit formation-based accent proposal tested here. My additions are the combined chronology, lexical screening, counterexample tests and stated alternative etymologies—not the discovery of the language data.

This remains an account of the evidence available for inspection, not a claim to have exhausted unpublished research. [Tegethoff's publication list](https://www.geschkult.fu-berlin.de/en/e/iranistik/mitarbeiter/drittmittel/tegethoff/index.html) records a 2025 thesis on Kalasha, Khowar and Western Shinaic historical phonology, and an article with Basu listed as in press. I could verify those bibliographic records but did not obtain their full texts. Kümmel’s 2015 talk, “Developments in the dissolution of the Indo-Iranian accentual system,” is also confirmed in the [ICHL programme](https://www.filmod.unina.it/immagini/2015.ProgrammaConvegnoLingSt.pdf), but I could not inspect its handout. These unavailable works may bear directly on some of the unresolved histories above.
