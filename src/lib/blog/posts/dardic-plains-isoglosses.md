How many other contrasts like this can we find? An exploratory script searched Jambu’s concept labels and accepted etymology links. **RAW, WOOL, MOUSTACHE, WATER and IRON** offer promising comparisons alongside LONG. **RIVER** offers another—and explains why a ranked list is only the beginning of the investigation.

The interactive charts below describe a frozen compiled-data snapshot analysed on **11 September 2026**. They show corpus evidence, not the percentage of speakers who use a word. The term *isogloss* suggests a boundary; what we have established here is a set of candidate lexical distributions. Drawing their geographical boundaries would require a separate examination of localities, dialects and actual usage.

## Finding the contrast without counting dictionaries

For this comparison, **Dardic** combines Jambu’s Chitrali, Kashmiric, Kohistani, Kunar, Pashai and Shinaic groups. **Plains** is a working label for a broad mainland comparison set: Hindi, Bihari, Eastern, Punjabi/Lahnda, Sindhic, Rajasthanic, Gujaratic, Bhil, Halbic and Marathi-Konkani groups. Pahari, insular, migratory and historical varieties are excluded, as is Domaaki. Nuristani is outside both groups. These choices define the experiment; they do not establish a genealogical unit or imply that every included language is spoken on a plain.

Each language gets **one vote**, regardless of how many sources or spellings it has. Records sharing a Glottocode count together. If a language has two resolved families for a concept, each gets half its vote; three families receive a third each. A language with no resolved family is kept in the coverage figures but cannot vote for an etymology. The script follows accepted ancestry to an Old Indo-Aryan head where available, and excludes paths marked as borrowing.

Unknown etymologies are neutral in the revised metric: they do not count as a different family or reduce the score. Positive support still matters. The score reaches full support once each focal family has five fractional language votes on its associated side; below that, it is scaled down.

That gives us a way to ask two questions together: is family A common on the Dardic side, and is family B common on the Plains side? A useful contrast also has relatively little crossover. The chart shows all four quantities, rather than only the two favourable ones. Each main concept viewer also has an inline map that follows its selected concept or comparison. Round outlines identify Dardic units and square outlines Plains units; split circles show family votes, and grey marks evidence without an included etymology. Click a point or use the language selector to inspect forms and unresolved records. The map keeps unknowns visible even when the bars use only linked languages.

## Six comparisons worth following

**RAW / UNRIPE: āma versus \*kacca.** This was prominent in the initial coverage-adjusted shortlist and remains a strong candidate with unknowns treated neutrally. For RAW, the āma family receives 74% of the Dardic vote and 9% of the Plains vote; \*kacca receives 79% on the Plains side and 3% on the Dardic side. UNRIPE produces a similar result, but often from the same records. It is corroborating semantic scope, not an independent discovery.

**WOOL: pakṣman versus ūrṇā.** The contrast connects superficially different Dardic forms such as *pām* and *paṣ* through their accepted family assignment. The shares are 59% for pakṣman on the Dardic side and 86% for ūrṇā on the Plains side. This is substantial, though less uniform in Dardic than the short list of examples might suggest.

**MOUSTACHE: \*phuṅga versus \*mucchā.** Neither focal family crosses into the opposite group in the resolved evidence used here. That makes this an especially attractive candidate for closer inspection. It is not universal: other families account for 27% of the linked Dardic vote and 23% of the linked Plains vote, and some languages remain unresolved.

**LONG: dīrgha versus lamba.** The starting example is recovered strongly: dīrgha receives 88% of the linked Dardic vote, while lamba receives 70% of the linked Plains vote. We will return to the crossover, which is essential to interpreting that result.

**WATER: udaka versus pānīya.** Udaka receives 64% of the Dardic vote and none of the resolved Plains vote in this snapshot. Pānīya receives 82% of the Plains vote, but also 16% of the Dardic vote. The contrast is therefore asymmetric: a Plains-associated family also has a meaningful Dardic presence.

**IRON: cīmara versus loha.** Cīmara dominates the resolved Dardic evidence at 96%. Loha receives 56% on the Plains side. Neither focal family crosses groups here, but fewer than half of the attested Plains language units have any resolved family. The striking contrast deserves attention without treating that missing evidence as agreement.

## Why RIVER missed the shortlist

With the coverage-adjusted metric applied to the current entry-family snapshot, RIVER is at **rank 138** among 1,743 positive contrasts, with **sindhu versus nadī** as its best pair. In the original concept-mapped sample, sindhu receives **46%** of the linked Dardic vote, nadī **81%** of the linked Plains vote. But nadī also receives **23%** of the Dardic vote, and only **13/22 Dardic** and **31/60 Plains** language units have resolved families. The first metric multiplied separation by coverage, so both crossover and incomplete coverage lowered its score. That treated lack of knowledge too much like lack of a contrast.

**The revised metric treats unknowns as neutral. RIVER rises from rank 138 to rank 70**, with a score of 0.519 rather than 0.287. Its focal families have six positive fractional votes on the Dardic side and 25 on the Plains side, enough for full support at the five-vote threshold. Its remaining limitations are known alternatives and real crossover, not the unlinked records. The lexical evidence itself is unchanged.

Among the other featured concepts, IRON rises from rank 21 to 7, LONG from 17 to 15, and WATER moves from 19 to 23 as other sparse-but-supported candidates rise. These are selected illustrations, not the new top seven. [Complete metric comparison for the discussed concepts](/research/dardic-plains/metric-comparison.csv).

Checking the underlying glosses reveals another problem. The RIVER concept includes an Odia record glossed “bank of pond or river,” a Saraiki record for inundation, and a Kalasha record for a precipice dropping into a stream or river. These are not straightforward words for a river. Concept matching has sometimes captured the surrounding definition instead of the thing being defined.

A narrow diagnostic keeps only records whose gloss **begins with “river” or “a river,” ignoring case**. Under that filter, sindhu’s Dardic share rises to **54%**, and nadī’s Plains share to **89%**. Nadī still receives **25%** of the Dardic vote. This check strengthens the comparison, but it does not turn it into an exceptionless boundary.

## How clean is clean enough?

A broad concept label can hide different senses. SOUR, another highly ranked candidate, includes “acid” and “sourness,” as well as the adjective “sour.” It needs a part-of-speech and sense review before it can support a clean adjective-to-adjective comparison. Related headwords can cause the opposite problem: separating derivatives can turn morphology into an apparent lexical replacement. The default entry-family mode groups numbered CDIAL subheads with their parent article only when an accepted explicit link supports the grouping. This includes some derivatives, so it is broader than strict variant identity. Explicitly linked variants already collapse in both modes. Unrelated derived articles, compounds and merely similar spellings are not automatically grouped. BIRCH contrasts bhūrja with its related *bhaurja subhead in separate-headword mode; the apparent opposition disappears when they count together. Every viewer offers the stricter mode, and the [collapse audit](/research/dardic-plains/family-collapse-audit.csv) lists every grouped head.

Coverage is a separate issue. A contrast can look extremely sharp among the available links while much of its attested sample remains unknown. Choose a concept and switch to Count to see the underlying denominators.

## Does earlier attestation favour the Dardic side?

**There is a descriptive tilt toward earlier-tagged families on the Dardic side, but most contrasts cannot be dated this way.** In collapsed entry-family mode, the 662 eligible concepts reduce to 648 distinct ordered family pairs. Of those, 389 have at least one family without a usable attestation-era tag. Among the remaining 259 pairs, the Dardic family has the earlier tag in **75**, the Plains family in **43**, and both have the same earliest era in **141**. Restricting to scores of at least 0.4 leaves 49 date-comparable pairs: **17 Dardic-earlier, 5 Plains-earlier, 27 tied**. Thus the direction is suggestive, but ties and missing dates dominate.

```chart
dardic-age
```

Here “age” means the **earliest era tag attached to a non-reconstructed Old Indo-Aryan head in Jambu**. Entry-family mode takes the earliest such tag among its member heads; separate-headword mode uses only that head. Reconstructed-only, undated OIA and other-origin families remain separate categories. Repeated concepts with the same ordered pair count once, at their highest score. Different pairs can still share heads, and the language samples are related, so these counts are not independent trials establishing a historical cause.

LONG illustrates the direction clearly in the metadata: [dīrgha](entry:6368) is tagged **Early Vedic**, while [lamba](entry:10951) is tagged **Epic**. But lamba’s cited meaning is “pendent,” so this is not a date for its use meaning “long.” WOOL and RIVER show why the pattern cannot be generalized: both focal heads in each pair carry Early Vedic tags. WATER is not a usable dated comparison here: pānīya is tagged Epic, but udaka lacks an era tag in this snapshot. That absence is a metadata gap, not evidence for late origin.

```chart
dardic-age-order
```

Separate-headword mode gives the same directional tendency: **62 Dardic-earlier, 36 Plains-earlier and 131 tied** among 229 date-comparable pairs; 426 further pairs lack a usable comparison. Collapsing families therefore increases date coverage as well as changing the lexical counts. Earlier textual attestation does not by itself prove uninterrupted inheritance, nor does later attestation date a word’s creation. The result motivates a closer review of particular replacements rather than a claim that Dardic always preserves the older word. ## What happens in the middle of the ranking?

**Middle scores mix several kinds of evidence.** With entry families collapsed, 31 eligible concepts score at least 0.6, 92 fall between 0.4 and 0.6, 140 between 0.2 and 0.4, and 399 below 0.2. These are concept labels, not independent discoveries: RAW/UNRIPE and COLUMN/POST can reuse evidence.

```chart
dardic-score-bands
```

The chart first separates cases that have fewer than five positive fractional votes for at least one focal family. Among the rest, it distinguishes substantial crossover from weak within-group concentration. The median support multiplier is **1.00** above 0.4, **0.70** in the 0.2–0.4 band, and **0.40** below 0.2. Low scores therefore often reflect insufficient positive support, even though unresolved records themselves have no penalty. The median sum of opposite-side focal shares is **11.1 percentage points** in the 0.4–0.6 band, compared with **3.6 points** in the highest band.

## Do Sindhi and Lahnda side with Dardic?

## What if only Dardic needs to be unified?

**This criterion recovers strong Dardic associations hidden by a diverse Plains vocabulary.** For each family A, take **max(0, D(A) − P(A)) × min(1, positive Dardic votes / 5)**, then choose the highest-scoring A. There is no required Plains family B. Both groups still need at least five linked language units, unknowns remain neutral, and any Plains occurrence of A reduces the score. The measure rewards Dardic concentration and distinctiveness together; it does not impose literal unanimity.

```chart
dardic-asymmetric
```

LAKE rises from **80th to 2nd among eligible concepts**, PATH from **49th to 6th**, and POST from **157th to 11th**. Their largest Plains families account for only 34%, 21% and 20%, respectively. For LAKE, the Plains evidence includes jhīl-type forms under *jhilla, tāl under talla and other pond/lake families; the opposing vocabulary is diverse even though Dardic saras is concentrated. COLUMN and POST share much of their pillar/post evidence, so their high scores should be interpreted together.

The change also promotes cautionary cases. DISTANCE rises from a two-sided score of **0.083 to 0.667**: dūra has all the linked Dardic vote, but also one third of the Plains vote, while the best opposing family has only one positive vote. This is no clean absence boundary, and the sample mixes “distant” with “distance.” The viewer includes both the top one-sided candidates and large rank gains with at least 70% Dardic concentration; click “Other resolved families” to inspect the Plains alternatives.

Switching back to separate heads exposes another sensitivity: BIRCH becomes a high one-sided candidate because bhūrja and its related *bhaurja subhead count separately. Collapsing them removes that apparent lexical opposition. Entry-family grouping is useful precisely because it can remove an attractive but misleading result. The new scores answer a different question and should be compared through their evidence and ranks, not treated as an increase in statistical confidence. ## Data and reproducibility

The revised ranking first selects the pair maximizing the mean of **D(A) − P(A)** and **P(B) − D(B)**, measured among linked language units. It multiplies this separation by **min(1, positive Dardic votes / 5, positive Plains votes / 5)**. A positive vote is the focal family’s fractional language contribution: a language with two resolved families supplies half a vote to each. The default eligibility threshold is five linked units in each group.

Adding unresolved records or unresolved-only languages leaves the score unchanged. Adding a known competing family can reduce its share; observing a focal family in the opposite group reduces separation. Coverage remains visible but has no multiplier. The old coverage-adjusted score is retained in the downloads so the effect of this choice can be inspected. This is a discovery heuristic, not a significance test; language votes are not genealogically independent. The River filter was added as a targeted diagnostic and was not used to rerank the complete concept list.

The [complete ranking of all 1,743 positive contrasts](/research/dardic-plains/all-concept-ranking.csv) makes the selection inspectable beyond the featured examples; filter `eligible` for the five-linked-language threshold. The frozen [analysis snapshot](/research/dardic-plains/snapshot.json) records settings, group membership and hashes of all five compiled input tables. The [evidence CSV](/research/dardic-plains/evidence.csv) contains the nine main comparison concepts, including UNRIPE and SOUR, with forms, glosses, family assignments and source references. [Language memberships](/research/dardic-plains/language-memberships.csv) and [weighted shares](/research/dardic-plains/weighted-shares.csv) make the denominators explicit. The [manifest](/research/dardic-plains/manifest.json) records checksums and verification results.

Map locations come from the frozen [language coordinate table](/research/dardic-plains/map-languages.csv), whose [provenance](/research/dardic-plains/coordinate-provenance.json) matches the lexical snapshot. They are representative language positions, not elicitation sites. A shared-Glottocode unit uses one located contributing language as its representative; units without coordinates remain in the bars and selector. The [map audit](/research/dardic-plains/map-points.csv) records each viewer’s categories, weights and positions.

The [analysis script](/research/dardic-plains/analyze_concept_isoglosses.py), together with its [graph helper](/research/dardic-plains/edges_util.py), reproduces the revised analysis against matching CLDF inputs. Figures are rebuilt from the frozen evidence by `research/dardic-plains/build.py` in the website repository; its assertions independently reconcile every family share and language denominator with the saved analysis. This post uses compiled data only: pending editorial overlays are not silently applied, and unmarked borrowing remains a possible source of error.

The four follow-up analyses are rebuilt by `research/dardic-plains/extensions.py` from frozen full-corpus results, evidence and compact head metadata in that directory’s `inputs/` package. The [extension manifest](/research/dardic-plains/extension-manifest.json) records its checksums. The [separate-headword ranking](/research/dardic-plains/head/all-concept-ranking.csv) and [evidence](/research/dardic-plains/head/evidence.csv) expose the stricter counting sensitivity; the charts and follow-up CSVs include both modes.

Full-corpus form evidence for all follow-up analyses is downloadable as compressed CSV in [entry-family mode](/research/dardic-plains/entry-full-evidence.csv.gz) and [separate-headword mode](/research/dardic-plains/head-full-evidence.csv.gz), including unresolved and excluded-loan records.
