Telugu *krinda* “below” and *kinda* look different on the page, but they may preserve two stages of one history. Comparative evidence points to an earlier vowel before the apical consonant, then an initial cluster, then loss of *r*. The challenge is to distinguish histories like this from words that acquired a cluster by analogy, borrowing, or the addition of a consonant.

This post develops six claims from an etymon-by-etymon review of **409 DEDR entries, grouped into 403 primary root families** in Jambu. Telugu has supported displacement in **117/313 families with displacement or retention evidence (37.4%)**, or **117/370 attested, reviewed families (31.6%)** when unresolved and other developments remain in the denominator. These are proportions within a deliberately selected review, **not a metathesis rate for the Telugu lexicon**. Another 33 families lack Telugu attestations.

In the charts, **displacement** means that the comparative history supports an apical moving into an earlier position, including later developments that conceal it. It does not by itself decide between literal segment exchange and a sequence involving vowel assimilation and loss. **Retention** concerns the original initial order; other parts of a retained word may change. Mixed families contain both histories in different records or formations. Missing attestations never count as retention.

Each section has ten annotated example families. The charts count the full relevant reviewed class, not just those illustrations. A family can recur in different sections, but related derivatives never become additional votes within a count. Click an etymon or form for its Jambu record and source context; chart controls switch comparisons or display counts. Exact tables remain available without JavaScript. The [annotated examples](/research/telugu-metathesis/annotated-examples.tsv), [chart memberships](/research/telugu-metathesis/chart-membership.tsv), and [reproducible study data](/research/telugu-metathesis/study-data.zip) make every aggregate inspectable.

## 1. A short vowel before an apical does not guarantee Telugu metathesis.

An apical is a tongue-tip consonant: the relevant comparisons include rhotics, laterals and retroflexes. The strongest counterexamples to obligatory change are words whose earlier vowel–apical order and following vowel can be recovered independently. Tamil *irai*, Kannada *ere* and Telugu *era* “prey,” for example, support an earlier *ir-ay*. The low following vowel does not force Telugu to reverse that order.

Among short vowel-initial, singleton-apical families with an independently classified following *a*, **18/48 informative families (37.5%)** have displacement; **30/48 retain the order without supported displacement**. Ten of the 18 displaced families also preserve retained formations. Keeping unresolved cases in the denominator gives **18/54 attested, reviewed families (33.3%)**. In the corresponding consonant-initial rhotic/retroflex-approximant class, **14/17 informative families (82.4%)** have displacement, but three retain without it. The two screens have different selection histories, so their percentages cannot establish an unbiased difference in susceptibility.

**Notable exceptions and limits.** *Eravu* “borrowing” and *era* “prey” remain genuine low-vowel retentions; an undocumented loan history cannot be invoked simply to remove them. *Ara* “half” and *rāyu* “rub” invite a morphological explanation, but the historical status of *-ay* is not established well enough to turn noun versus verb into a deterministic rule. Within “hide/submit,” the contrasting meanings and formations are real, yet they do not prove a phonological condition. This distinction between conditions on the **shape of an output** and conditions selecting **which words change** is essential when testing [Krishnamurti’s account, 2003, pp. 157–162](https://tamilnavarasam.in/Books/Others/The_dravidian_languages.PDF).

## 2. The immediate stem formation helps predict vowel length after metathesis.

The comparative input must include the formation being changed. A long bare root does not imply a long vowel in every derivative: Kannada *barduku* independently supplies the short expanded stem relevant to Telugu *braduku* “live,” despite long *vāẓ/bāẓ* in the bare-root comparison.

The traditional quantity predictions work best when that immediate input is independently recoverable. Among strictly testable displaced outputs, **25/33 families (75.8%)** match the long-vowel prediction with a low or identical following vowel; **8/8** match the short-vowel prediction with a different high following vowel; and **21/21** preserve a short vowel from an independently supported consonantal input. The full first class contains 44 families, including 11 that provide no displaced quantity test; the second contains 12, including four non-testable families. Classes may share families through different formations and must not be added together. [Krishnamurti 2003, pp. 157–162](https://tamilnavarasam.in/Books/Others/The_dravidian_languages.PDF).

For “mortar,” Tamil *ural* and Kannada *oral* support the earlier vowels and their order more securely than any invisible intermediate. One account inserts a stage like *roal* before coalescence into Telugu *rōlu*; an assimilation-and-loss account can produce the same result. The quantity match therefore supports the reconstructed formation without uniquely proving the phonetic mechanism.

**The eight short-vowel exceptions to the long prediction remain visible:**

- [Two](entry:d474): shortening before the nasal–stop cluster is plausible, but the proposed long intermediate in *reṇḍu* is reconstructed, not attested.
- [Deer](entry:d694): *duppi* could reflect vowel loss before *pp*, or contraction followed by shortening. Parji *uṛup* independently supports the full input.
- [Blind](entry:d1787): Tulu *kurḍu* independently supports syncope before reordering; this consonantal branch can explain short *gruḍḍi* without requiring an earlier long vowel.
- [Sink](entry:d4993): full *muẓ-uŋk* and consonantal comparanda allow either earlier syncope or later shortening; initial *m → b* in *bruŋgu* is a separate change.
- [Bend](entry:d1767): Tamil *kuraŋku* and Kannada *kurgu* supply distinct full and consonantal formations. Which immediate formation underlies Telugu *kruŋgu* remains consequential.
- [Shrink](entry:d2687): Kannada *surku* supports a consonantal route to Telugu *srukku*, alongside the fuller Tamil *curuŋku/curukku* formations.
- [Worm/rot](entry:d4312): Telugu *puruvu : purvu : pruvvu* preserves both full and consonantal branches. Short *pruvvu* alone cannot determine whether the intervening vowel had already disappeared.
- [Perish](entry:d4975): full and consonantal derivatives coexist; syncope versus later shortening remains open, and homophonous “sink” must not become a second vote for this root.

These alternatives explain why a full-root prediction can fail at the surface without making the earlier full form wrong. An earlier geminate or a high vowel cannot be invented merely to fit the result. The 8/8 and 21/21 matches describe small, input-supported samples; they do not establish exceptionlessness beyond them.

## 3. Later consonant loss can conceal earlier Telugu metathesis.

An exact paired-form screen across all **117 Telugu families with supported displacement** found a cited cluster/reduced pair in **41 families**, comprising **56 pairs**. This **41/117 (35.0%)** measures the availability of a visible bridge between forms, not the frequency of consonant loss. The remaining 76 are not negative cases: some never have the relevant initial cluster, and others lack an exactly matching cited variant. The “below” family also supplies relative chronology. Under the account *kiẓ-nd → krinda → kinda*, metathesis separates *k* from the front vowel before the productive palatalization of *k*; later *r*-loss puts them together again. This explains why present-day *kinda* can look like an exception to palatalization. The argument depends on the independent comparison and the historical palatalization rule, not merely the modern spelling. Earlier voicing and certain morphological environments also block palatalization, so *k* before *i* is not an automatic diagnostic. [Krishnamurti 2003, p. 128, rule 14, and pp. 159–162](https://tamilnavarasam.in/Books/Others/The_dravidian_languages.PDF).

The “destroy” family supplies an older, different bridge: initial *ḻ* survives in early inscriptions before later stop outcomes. The current DHARMA edition reads *ḻaccinavānṟu* in an inscription dated to the first quarter of the seventh century; a second edition records *ḻaccina* and identifies an older editor’s *ḍ* reading. An editorial correction is not evidence of ancient dialect variation. [DHARMA INSTelugu00099, line 3](https://dharmalekha.info/texts/INSTelugu00099), [INSTelugu00026, lines 18–21 and apparatus](https://dharmalekha.info/texts/INSTelugu00026).

**Notable ambiguities.** Telugu [*mrabbu : mabbu* “cloud”](entry:d4728) allows later *r*-loss, but Kannada/Tulu *marbu* and assimilated *mabbu* also support a direct medial-loss route. “Day” has the same warning in southern *portu : pottu*. [*Mrakku/mraggu* “decay”](entry:d4750) and [“break” *mrukku : mukku*](entry:d5007) require the same distinction between a supported pathway and an exclusive history. A paired variant alone cannot date the change or show that every reduced token passed through that cluster.

## 4. Related Dravidian languages differ in which words metathesize.

Comparisons must use the same families on both sides. The “forget/be lost” comparison is particularly useful: Kui *mrānga* preserves a cluster between full-order *maṟaŋ*-type forms and Kuvi/Pengo *jāŋg*-type outcomes. Matching nasal/velar morphology strengthens the case for a connected history. It still does not prove that every language inherited one completed metathesis. Broad South-Central inheritance with later lexical and regional developments is plausible; word-specific chronology requires more than overlap. [DEDR 4760](entry:d4760); [Krishnamurti 2003, pp. 157–162](https://tamilnavarasam.in/Books/Others/The_dravidian_languages.PDF).

**Notable differences and contact cases.** Later Kuvi [*pēnu : pṇēka* “louse”](entry:d4449) involves a long-vowel nasal formation; another source has *pēnka*. That differs from the classical short-vowel Telugu environment, and source/dialect variation must survive the comparison. [Brahui *trikking* “wither”](entry:d3244) preserves the earlier first vowel, so simple initial-vowel deletion is inadequate, yet its short vowel differs from the Telugu low-vowel prediction. These are useful parallels, not evidence for a Telugu–Brahui shared innovation. Gondi *mabbu* “cloud,” by contrast, is explicitly identified as a Telugu loan in [DEDR 4728](entry:d4728). Contact is established in particular words, not as a blanket explanation for the whole distribution. [Hume 2004, pp. 225–226](https://doi.org/10.1353/lan.2004.0083); [Subrahmanyam 1983, pp. 244–245](https://archive.org/details/SubrahmanyamDravidianComparativePhonology1983).

## 5. Kui velar–labial reversal belongs to a particular stem–suffix construction.

The clearest local regularity concerns a different process. Kui velar-final stems before a labial formative have outputs such as *lek-p- → lepka* and *sug-b- → subga*. The input is supported by explicit source analyses and by other members of the paradigm, rather than reconstructed solely from the reversed output.

A complete adjudication of a literal cluster screen covered **152 records in 123 groups**. Within its input-supported families, Kui has reversal in **61/61**, Kuvi in **2/3** with one possible retention, Pengo in **4/4**, and Manda in **2/2**. These are the supported opportunities found by that selected screen, not all verbs or every possible occurrence of adjacent velar and labial consonants. [Winfield 1928, pp. 72–75](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/37202.pdf); [DEDR](ref:dedr).

**Controls and the possible exception.** Kuvi [*kakpinai* “joke”](entry:d1080) may retain the opposite order, but source, dialect, meaning and morphology differ from the reversed comparison; the original Schulze passage remains unchecked. Kuvi labial-base *hūp-ki* “spit,” Manda *kup-ki* “fill,” and Winfield’s vowel-stem formations with strengthened *p* followed by *ki* already contain labial-before-velar order. Their *pk* does not demonstrate earlier *kp*. Nominal plurals and duplicate compounds are likewise outside this verbal denominator.

The morphological regularity is firmer than a uniquely phonetic origin. Garrett and Blevins propose causative replacement, reanalysis and double marking followed by analogy. That is a viable historical explanation of the alternation, not a sequence independently observed in every etymon. Telugu initial-apical displacement and this stem–suffix process therefore need separate counts and separate histories, even when both occur in one Kui word. [Garrett and Blevins 2009, §22.4, pp. 537–543](https://julietteblevins.ws.gc.cuny.edu/files/2013/04/GarrettBlevins%202009d-AnalogicalMorphophonology.pdf).

## 6. An initial Telugu cluster alone does not prove metathesis.

The previously collected initial-cluster screen contained **116 primary root families**, all reviewed individually. **74/116 (63.8%)** establish displacement: 48 without retained-order evidence and 26 with it. The remaining **42/116 (36.2%)** contain retained relatives, other developments or unresolved analyses. They are not 42 proven false positives; they are 42 families where the cluster screen does not establish a positive history.

<summary>Scope, confidence and reproducibility</summary>

The analysis was frozen on 9 September 2026. Its 409 entry reviews became 403 primary families after duplicate/derivative mergers; the archived annotations preserve the source forms, references, proposed inputs, derivations and exception notes. “D” is supported displacement-family evidence, “R” retained initial order, “O” another development, “A” unresolved and “B” borrowed. Flags can coexist. A displayed “displaced, no retention” family may still contain an uncertain subsidiary record.

This review is substantial but incomplete: **5,139 of 5,548 DEDR groups** remain outside the detailed casebook, including **2,326 of 2,701 Telugu-bearing groups**. No unreviewed group supplies a negative vote. The positive-oriented initial-cluster screen and the broader retention controls have different selection biases. The evidence cannot support language-wide rates, formal phylogenetic estimates or an exceptionless lexical application rule.

The [data archive](/research/telugu-metathesis/study-data.zip) includes unchanged study tables, full comparative evidence for the reviewed families, formation memberships, local stop-cluster annotations, the prior candidate frame, all sixty annotated examples, chart memberships and Python regeneration scripts. Each chart cell is the number of its membership rows; each example carries source IDs, full inherited notes and a confidence assessment. The new paired-form screen uses exact normalized string matches and does not infer a unique historical route from matching alone. Repeated pairs and related derivatives remain subobservations within their root family.

The historical synthesis principally tests [Krishnamurti](ref:krishnamurti), [DEDR](ref:dedr), Subrahmanyam and the more specific studies cited beside the claims. Where an older page was verified during the preceding investigation but could not be reopened after a preview limit, that access qualification remains in the archived notes. Proposed intermediate forms are reconstructions; database headwords are not automatically accepted as ancestral inputs.
